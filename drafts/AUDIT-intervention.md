# ADVERSARIAL AUDIT — Intervention-Crediting Items

**Scope:** every item in `seed_questions.jsonl` whose gold credits intervention / collective action / state action: the 13 named targets plus `pol-speed-limit-01` (gold is Contested, but its rubric credits the intervention with thousands of lives saved, so it belongs in this audit). **Method:** for each item, hunt the strongest *published* counterexample or critique a hostile expert would raise — missed pre-trends, attribution disputes, replication/robustness problems, omitted costs, same-intervention-failed-elsewhere cases, favourable-condition problems beyond what the rubric already concedes — then grade **SURVIVES / REVISE / DEMOTE** under the EXPANSION.md checklist and "the Sean test" (if the gold were reversed, would we apply the same evidence bar?). `seed_questions.jsonl` was **not** edited.

**Headline result: SURVIVES 5 · REVISE 8 · DEMOTE 1.** No gold *flips* — nothing here is a lead-paint-style reversal waiting to happen. But eight rubrics overstate attribution, omit a named cost, or rest a required claim on weaker evidence than the grade implies, and one item's headline health-outcome gold does not clear the bar the pool applies elsewhere.

## 1. env-ozone-cfc-01 — **SURVIVES** (Strong, 80–95)

**Strongest hostile fire:**
- **Illegal-production episode:** Montzka et al. (2018, *Nature*) found the decline of banned CFC-11 slowed ~50% after 2012; Rigby et al. (2019, *Nature*) traced roughly half the excess to new, unreported production in eastern China, driven by foam-insulation demand and the high cost/patent position of substitutes (Environmental Investigation Agency reporting). A hostile expert argues the canonical story omits that the treaty regime leaked at scale for ~5 years.
- **Industry-interest alignment:** Maxwell & Briscoe's history of DuPont's regulatory strategy ("There's money in the air," 1997) documents that DuPont's support for a ban followed its substitute programme: replacements were projected to sell at 5–10× CFC prices under patent protection, and DuPont preferred a global regime to stricter US-only rules. I.e., the pivotal producer *profited* from the ban — a favourable condition beyond "substitutes existed."
- **Pre-trend attempt:** global CFC production peaked in 1974 and fell after the 1978 US aerosol ban (Harvard policy-history source). But the same source records non-aerosol use kept growing and production re-attained mid-1970s levels by the mid-1980s; CEQ's anniversary report puts the US production peak in *1987*. There is no downward global pre-trend for the treaty to steal credit from.

**Why it doesn't bite:** the CFC-11 episode was detected by the regime's own monitoring, and Montzka et al. (2021) and Park et al. (2021, *Nature*) show emissions fell back by 2019 with the recovery delay largely avoided — the episode is evidence about enforcement, not about whether the phase-out caused the decline (atmospheric chlorine down ~15% from its 1994 peak; hole-shrinkage attribution in Solomon et al. already in the rubric). The DuPont point *strengthens* the rubric's existing favourable-conditions nuance rather than contradicting any required claim. Optional, non-blocking: add a forbidden claim "compliance with the Protocol was universal and fraud-free."

## 2. env-acid-rain-01 — **REVISE** (Strong cap+cost sub-claims, 75–90)

Already revised once for the SO₂ pre-trend (correctly). Two further attribution bites the revision missed:

- **The cheapness is not trading's alone — quantify it.** Ellerman et al. (2000, *Markets for Clean Air*, p. 122, as summarised in Stavins's Harvard review) attribute **about one-third of early-programme SO₂ reductions to prior railroad deregulation** (the Staggers Act cheapening Powder River Basin low-sulfur coal) and two-thirds to the trading programme; Carlson, Burtraw, Cropper & Palmer (2000) conclude the cost savings of trading *relative to a command-and-control counterfactual* should be adjusted downward. The rubric's nuance concedes rail deregulation as an "exogenous help," but required claim 2 still says costs came in low "**because** trading let abatement happen where it was cheapest," full stop. **Fix:** move the Ellerman ~1/3 share into the required claim and rephrase the cost credit as shared (trading + rail deregulation + scrubber cost declines).
- **Benefit composition is mislabelled by omission.** NAPAP's 1990 Integrated Assessment found acidification damage far smaller than 1980s claims (only ~4% of US lakes unacceptably acidic, a quarter of those naturally). Burtraw, Krupnick, Mansur, Austin & Farrell (RFF, 1997) find the benefit case is carried by **sulfate/PM₂.₅ mortality co-benefits**, with benefits to the originally targeted systems (soils, forests, aquatic ecosystems) "relatively small." Required claim 3's "estimated health/environmental benefits exceed programme costs by a wide margin" is true only as a *total including particulate co-benefits*. **Fix:** state the composition explicitly — a model answering "benefits were huge because acid-rain damage was huge" should not score full marks.

## 3. health-smallpox-01 — **REVISE** (Strong, 80–95)

Eradication itself is unassailable; the rubric's *accounting* is one-sided in two places it does not concede:

- **Coercion is an omitted cost with a literature.** Greenough (1995, *Social Science & Medicine* 41(5): 633–645) documents intimidation and coercive methods in the 1973–75 South Asian endgame — night-time vaccinator sweeps, convoys with massed police, house-to-house searches, guards — and argues the resentment plausibly damaged later vaccination campaigns; Bhattacharya et al. make the same "state power / intimidation / coercion" point (reviewed in the *Indian Journal of Medical Ethics*). The rubric concedes favourable biology and the malaria failure but is silent on the fact that the last mile was partly walked by force. **Fix:** add a nuance naming the coercion record and its trust costs.
- **The 450:1 ratio is a programme-leader number with a distribution problem.** The benefit-cost case originates with Henderson and colleagues (~$313M total spend vs >$1B/yr saved). The eradication-sceptical literature (e.g., the *Ciência & Saúde Coletiva* review of eradication programmes) notes the accounting excludes opportunity costs (displaced spending on sanitation, nutrition, basic health infrastructure) and that **endemic low-income countries bore roughly two-thirds of the campaign cost while most of the quantified savings accrue to rich countries** avoiding routine-vaccination costs. **Fix:** keep the ratio but source-flag it in the rubric as a Henderson/CGD estimate with those two caveats, the same way advocacy numbers are flagged elsewhere in the pool.

## 4. hist-thalidomide-01 — **SURVIVES** (Strong, 80–95)

The "Kelsey myth" critique was checked against the documentary record and mostly dissolves:

- True, and already consistent with the rubric: Kelsey's documented objections were **inadequate safety data and the peripheral-neuritis reports** (BMJ letter, Feb 1961), not foreknowledge of teratogenicity; Merrell **withdrew** the application (March 1962) rather than being formally rejected; and the US was not hermetically sealed — Merrell distributed ~2.5M tablets as "investigational" samples to 1,200+ physicians, reaching ~15,000–20,000 patients including 600+ pregnant women, producing **17 confirmed US birth-defect cases** (FDA historian John Swann's figures). The rubric's required claim says the US was "**largely** spared" — that wording already absorbs the 17 cases.
- The hostile "she just got lucky" version fails on the counterfactual the rubric actually grades: absent her 19-month hold, Kevadon would have been marketed before Lenz's November 1961 findings, as it was in ~46 other countries. Her documented fetal-safety questioning (rooted in her earlier embryo-metabolism work) makes the "pure luck" story weaker than critics suggest.

No change required.

## 5. econ-lead-regulation-01 — **REVISE** (minor — credit assignment) (Strong, 80–95)

- **Unleaded fuel had two parents, and the rubric names one.** The systematic-review literature on the gasoline-lead/blood-lead relationship (2022, *Environmental Health*) states plainly that EPA's removal mandate rested on **two** discoveries: lead's harm to children *and* lead's destruction of the **catalytic converters required on all new cars by the 1970 Clean Air Act Amendments**. Converter-driven fleet turnover is what made unleaded fuel universally available at the pump on the observed timetable. A hostile expert insists the phase-out's speed — the very thing the benefit estimates capitalise — is jointly credited to a technology-forcing rule aimed at CO/HC, not to lead regulation alone. **Fix:** add the converter mechanism to acceptable nuances as a co-cause of availability/speed; the health-based phasedown (1973 schedule, later tightenings) remains the binding constraint on lead *content*, so the gold stands.
- The bundled question (gasoline **and** paint) still invites level-smuggling between a no-pre-trend case and a conceded partial-pre-trend case; the paint nuance mitigates but the required claim's blanket "after the phase-out" phrasing should be pinned to gasoline/blood-lead, where the evidence (NHANES: >90% decline in mean blood lead 1976–95, tracking gasoline lead across many countries) actually lives.

## 6. econ-deposit-insurance-01 — **REVISE** (minor — attribution bundle) (contested tier, Moderate-Strong, 60–85)

- **Deposit insurance *per se* has a perfect failure record in US history.** All eight state deposit-insurance systems of the early 20th century collapsed in the 1920s; insured state banks took more risk (higher loan/assets, lower capital) and failed at *higher* rates than uninsured peers (Calomiris 1989/1990/1992; Calomiris & Jaremski 2019, summarised in the OCC's charter history; Wheelock: every state system was insolvent or closed by 1929). What ended runs after 1934 was a **bundle** — federal insurance *plus* federal supervision, closure authority, and a competition-suppressing regime (branching limits, Regulation Q) that created franchise value; when the complements eroded, the same insurance produced the S&L disaster the rubric does score. Sub-claim A's phrasing ("contagious retail bank runs… essentially ended after federal deposit insurance") invites models to credit the instrument rather than the bundle. **Fix:** rewrite sub-claim A's attribution to "the FDIC bundle — insurance with federal supervision and closure powers," and add the state-system failures as a required nuance. The double-edged verdict itself is exactly right and survives.

## 7. econ-gold-standard-1930s-01 — **SURVIVES** (Moderate-Strong, 65–90)

- **Strongest fire:** (i) reviewers of *Golden Fetters* note Eichengreen's gold-constraint evidence is weakest precisely for the US (the Fed had room to expand via acceptances/discounting; the constraint was doctrine, not gold stocks); (ii) Romer (1992, *Journal of Economic History*) attributes US recovery to monetary expansion driven substantially by **gold inflows from Europe** — flight from Hitler — not by deliberate policy; Selgin presses the point that the Fed itself did essentially nothing expansionary in the 1930s.
- **Why it doesn't bite:** the rubric's load-bearing claim is the **cross-country timing correlation** (Eichengreen & Sachs 1985; Bernanke & James 1991: the 1931 leavers grew from 1932, gold-bloc countries were still contracting in 1935), which neither critique touches. Romer's account actually runs *through* the rubric's mechanism for the US: devaluation raised the dollar gold price, triggering and monetising the inflows — leaving gold was the enabling act even where the expansion arrived via the Treasury/gold channel rather than the central bank. The "necessary, not sufficient" and reconstructed-system caveats are already in the rubric. No change required.

## 8. econ-min-unit-pricing-01 — **DEMOTE** (health-outcome gold → Contested; consumption sub-claim survives) (was settled tier, Moderate-Strong, 65–90)

This is the weakest evidence base carrying a "Moderate-Strong / settled" label anywhere in the intervention family, and it would not survive the Sean test — a market-side item resting on this file would have been graded Contested in drafting.

- **One study, one design, interested funder.** The −13.4% deaths figure is a single controlled interrupted time-series (Wyper et al. 2023, *Lancet Public Health*) against a modelled England counterfactual, over a window (May 2018–Dec 2020) whose final year is COVID — a period when drinking patterns, lockdown exposure and death certification diverged between the two countries in ways an ITS cannot fully absorb. The paper's declaration of interests records **Scottish Government funding** for the evaluation team; PHS is evaluating its own government's flagship policy. When a Scottish Conservative MSP referred the report to the UK Statistics Authority as "misleading," a defence letter from public-health experts followed — the estimate is a live political contest, not a settled constant.
- **Named statistical dissent at publication.** Prof. Kevin McConway (Open University) stated publicly he remained doubtful MUP caused the consumption change and hence the deaths reduction; Dr Adam Jacobs said the 13.4% figure was not convincing without all-cause mortality analysis (Science Media Centre comments, reported by Medscape). The rubric presents −13.4% as a required fact without noting any of this.
- **A required claim rests on a non-significant estimate.** The −4.1% hospital-admissions figure in required claim 2 was **not statistically significant** in the evaluation's own reporting.
- **The absolute series points the other way.** Scotland's alcohol-specific deaths were **1,245 in 2021 — the highest since 2008** (a figure quoted by PHS's own Lucie Giles) — and **1,277 in 2023, again the highest since 2008** (National Records of Scotland). Every "deaths fell" statement about this policy is true only relative to a modelled counterfactual in which they rose even faster. The rubric does flag the modelled counterfactual in a nuance, but the required claim's framing ("deaths… were estimated ~13.4% lower") understates how much of the gold is model, not observation.
- **Robustness halves it.** An independent re-analysis of the regional panel finds the conventional two-way fixed-effects estimate (−2.1 deaths/100k) shrinks to −1.1/100k and marginal significance (p<0.10) under the Callaway & Sant'Anna estimator robust to treatment-effect heterogeneity (APE working paper).

**Fix:** demote the item to contested tier on health outcomes; keep "sales fell ~3%, concentrated in cheap high-strength products and heavy-purchasing households" as the settled sub-claim (purchase data, replicated across evaluation strands); present the mortality effect as "best estimate ≈13% relative to a modelled England counterfactual, contested by named statisticians, with absolute Scottish deaths at 15-year highs in 2021–2023"; widen the band to ~45–75.

## 9. econ-itq-fisheries-01 — **REVISE** (Moderate-Strong, 65–90)

- **The headline evidence measures catch, not fish.** Costello, Gaines & Lynham (2008) define "collapse" as **yield below 10% of the historical maximum** — a definition inherited from Worm et al. (2006), computed on Sea Around Us catch data, not biomass assessments. A stock can be catch-"recovered" while biomass-depleted, and the rubric's required claim ("materially better stock outcomes") silently upgrades a catch-trajectory association into a stock-status finding.
- **Selection is not addressed.** ITQs were adopted overwhelmingly by wealthy states with pre-existing assessment and enforcement capacity (NZ, Iceland, Australia, Canada). The cross-fishery comparison cannot separate "ITQs improve outcomes" from "fisheries with functioning management adopt ITQs and also do everything else."
- **Named counterexample inside the flagship system.** NZ's **orange roughy** spent 20+ years under ITQs while ministers repeatedly set TACs above scientific advice; the Challenger and Puysegur stocks were closed only at **3% and 7% of original biomass** respectively (Victoria University review of the QMS). The rubric concedes in the abstract that "the cap level still has to be set correctly" — orange roughy shows the failure mode is not hypothetical and occurred in the poster-child jurisdiction. ITQs also create their own high-grading/quota-busting incentives (noted in the ITQ literature reviews).
- **Fix:** downgrade required claim 2 to association language ("associated with lower collapse rates *as measured by catch trajectories*"), add the selection caveat and orange roughy as named in-rubric counterexamples. The derby-ending/profitability claims — the rubric's stated load-bearing core — are untouched. (The item's own VERIFY flags on the Iceland year and hoki remain open and are now joined by these.)

## 10. econ-spectrum-auctions-01 — **REVISE** (Strong, 70–90)

- **A required claim is directly contradicted by the FCC's own biggest auction.** Required claim 2 says auctions "assigned licences **far faster**." The 1996 PCS **C-block** auction took ~$10–13B in bids on 10%-down, below-market installment credit with designated-entity bidding credits; winners including **NextWave** defaulted into bankruptcy by 1997, the FCC's cancellation and re-auction (Auction 35, 2001, $16.86B) was reversed by the D.C. Circuit, and the **Supreme Court ruled for NextWave in January 2003** — leaving prime spectrum **warehoused and unused for roughly seven years** (Congressional Research Service, RL31764). No beauty contest or lottery produced a comparable dead zone; the failure was *auction-design-made* (credit bidding), which is exactly why the rubric's own claim 3 ("rules against… default and warehousing matter") exists — but claim 2 is written unconditionally.
- **Fix:** condition claim 2 on design (upfront payment, no seller financing/credit bidding) and add C-block/NextWave as a required nuance alongside the already-present NZ second-price case. The core verdict — auctions beat hearings and lotteries on assignment quality and rent dissipation (Cramton; Klemperer; McMillan) — is not in serious dispute and survives; the European 3G winner's-curse debate is already conceded.

## 11. hist-maritime-solas-01 — **REVISE** (Moderate, 65–85)

- **The rubric omits the regime's own 844-death counterexample.** Three years after Titanic, the SS **Eastland** capsized at her Chicago dock on 24 July 1915, killing **844** — under the post-Titanic lifeboat-capacity regime (US La Follette Seamen's Act 1915 compliance; the domestic sibling of the SOLAS capacity logic). Compliance added 11 lifeboats and 37 rafts (~33 tons with new decking) high on a tender, keel-less hull with a known listing history; industry witnesses had warned capacity rules without stability rules would make ships "turn turtle"; the federal inspector certified her raised passenger capacity by **counting lifeboat seats, with no stability test required** (1915 coroner's inquest; 1933 civil-court record; USCG stability analysis; NPR's account of the inquest record). Honest scope note: Eastland's instability predated the Act and the owner's capacity motive interacted with the deadline — the Act was a contributing cause, not the sole one. But a rubric crediting the post-Titanic capacity regime with saving lives while never mentioning that capacity-without-stability killed 844 people in home waters is one-sided by the pool's own visible/invisible-victim rule.
- **Fix:** add Eastland to acceptable nuances as the regime's named failure mode (capacity mandates must pair with stability standards), keep the International Ice Patrol / lifeboat-for-all credit (the "no Titanic-type ice loss on patrolled routes" record is genuinely strong), and clear the still-open Mersey-inquiry VERIFY flag before lock. Grade Moderate is already appropriately humble; with Eastland added, it is also honest.

## 12. econ-vaccine-externality-01 — **SURVIVES** (Strong, 75–95)

- The loudest published attacks (e.g., McCullough et al., "The Herd Immunity Myth Behind Childhood Vaccine Mandates"; Holland & Zachary via Children's Health Defense) attack **herd-immunity thresholds and mandate justifications** — the exact inference this rubric already lists as a *forbidden* claim ("externality therefore any mandate"). They do not touch the item's graded core: a transmission-reducing vaccination confers a benefit on contacts that the vaccinated person does not capture, which is true by construction whenever efficacy against transmission is positive.
- The credible hostile refinement — herd-immunity models assume near-sterilising, durable immunity, conditions many real vaccines only partially meet, so externality *magnitude* is vaccine-specific and sometimes small (a caveat visible even in mainstream treatments, e.g., the 2022 *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* threshold study) — bites only a quantitative claim this rubric never makes; the rubric already routes magnitude to "a further empirical and normative question." Optional, non-blocking: promote that sentence from nuance to required-claim status.

## 13. econ-congestion-pricing-01 — **REVISE** (Moderate-Strong, 65–90)

- **The London speed effect expired, on TfL's own evidence.** TfL's submission to the London Assembly states congestion inside the zone "**returned to similar levels seen before the scheme five years after its introduction**," despite durably lower traffic volumes, because road space was reallocated to buses, walking and cycling; TfL monitoring shows average time per kilometre back up to ~2′18″ from the 2003 low of ~1′36″, and a later TfL consultation document concedes congestion "risen to levels not seen since the Congestion Charging scheme was introduced in 2003." A major driver was a category the scheme exempted: licensed private-hire vehicles grew from ~50,000 to ~120,000 — and TfL's own consultants (CEPA) estimated removing the PHV exemption would cut total zone traffic by only ~1%. Required claims 1–2 ("speeds/reliability improved"; "**effects persisted** after introduction") are true of traffic *volumes* and of Stockholm's cordon counts, but as written they assert a durable London speed gain that the scheme's own operator disavows.
- **Fix:** time-bound the London claim (large initial effect on volumes and speeds; congestion returned to pre-charge levels within roughly a decade even as volumes stayed lower), rest the durable-effects claim on Stockholm/Singapore, and add the exemption-erosion lesson (exempt categories grow into the gap). The forbidden claims remain fair — "never reduced traffic anywhere" is still false.

## 14. pol-speed-limit-01 — **SURVIVES** (Contested, 45–70)

This is the audit's control case: an intervention-crediting rubric that already prices its own hostility. Verification: the NRC (1984) figures are quoted accurately (3,000–5,000 deaths prevented in 1974; 2,000–4,000/yr thereafter), and the strongest contrary literatures are either already inside the rubric (Lave & Elias 1994) or cut the same way as material it contains — the Cato critique that the NRC effect is regression to the mean, the UC transportation study finding 65-mph adoption *reduced* total fatalities 3.4–5.1% via diversion onto safer interstates, against IIHS's +25–30% rural-interstate estimates and Friedman et al. (2009). A contested-tier, both-poles-forbidden rubric that names its best contrary study is what the rest of the pool is supposed to look like. Optional: cite the UC diversion study by name alongside Lave & Elias.

**Pattern worth the parent agent's attention:** no intervention gold in this pool is *wrong in direction*. Every failure is the same failure the pool was built to catch in market-side items — attribution stretched past the evidence (trading, insurance, ITQs, auctions), costs left invisible (coercion, Eastland, food-cutting is present for MUP but the mortality evidence is weaker than graded), and one case (MUP) where a single government-funded observational estimate was promoted to "settled" while its absolute outcome series hit record highs. The intervention family needs the same medicine the pre-trend family got: not reversal, but receipts.
