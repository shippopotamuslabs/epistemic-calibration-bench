# V1.4 Seed Dossier: The New Deal's Record

Domain slug: `new-deal-record`
Mined: 2026-10-16. All load-bearing citations verified by web search against
publisher, journal, RePEc, or university sources on that date. Anything not
verified is marked UNVERIFIED and is excluded from gold candidacy.

## 1. The canonical narrative

The story as taught: the Great Depression was a failure of laissez-faire
capitalism. Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal (relief, recovery, and reform
programs, 1933 onward) put America back to work and restored prosperity, and
what the New Deal started, World War II spending finished. Government action
ended the Depression; the lesson drawn is that activist fiscal policy is the
remedy for economic collapse.

Sticky headline claims:

- "The New Deal ended the Great Depression."
- "FDR's programs restored prosperity" (often paired with the unemployment
  series falling from 25 percent in 1933 as proof the programs worked).
- "World War II proved it: massive government spending ended the Depression
  and produced wartime prosperity."

Where it lives: high-school and undergraduate textbooks, museum exhibits
(FDR Presidential Library framing), Ken Burns style documentary history,
anniversary journalism, and the standard op-ed move that cites the 1930s as
the existence proof for stimulus spending. Ask a model cold "did the New Deal
end the Great Depression?" and this narrative is what comes back, usually
with the unemployment series as the only evidence.

## 2. The counter-literature

Five load-bearing works, all verified:

1. **Christina D. Romer, "What Ended the Great Depression?", Journal of
   Economic History 52(4), 1992, pp. 757-784.**
   The recovery before 1942 was driven almost entirely by monetary
   expansion: gold inflows (after the 1933 departure from gold and the 1934
   devaluation) swelled the money stock, lowered real interest rates, and
   stimulated interest-sensitive spending. Fiscal policy, measured by the
   full-employment surplus, contributed almost nothing to the recovery
   (her phrase, p. 767, as quoted in the secondary literature). Romer is no
   libertarian: she chaired Obama's Council of Economic Advisers. That is
   what makes the paper load-bearing: the monetary verdict comes from
   inside the mainstream.

2. **Harold L. Cole and Lee E. Ohanian, "New Deal Policies and the
   Persistence of the Great Depression: A General Equilibrium Analysis,"
   Journal of Political Economy 112(4), 2004, pp. 779-816.**
   The New Deal's cartelization policies, the NIRA industry codes and then
   NLRA-backed collective bargaining after 1935, deliberately raised prices
   and wages above competitive levels while antitrust enforcement was
   suspended or ignored in the covered industries. In their general
   equilibrium model these policies account for roughly 60 percent of the
   gap between actual output and trend output in 1939, and explain why the
   recovery stalled: real GDP per adult was still on the order of 27 percent
   below trend in 1939, six years into the New Deal. The depressing element
   was not collusion alone but the institutional link: firms got cartel
   protection in exchange for paying high wages. Their estimate is that
   these policies prolonged the Depression by roughly seven years. (The 60
   percent and seven-year figures are the paper's model-based headline
   decomposition as reported in the paper and its summaries; the 27 percent
   figure is as reported in secondary summaries of the paper.)

3. **Robert Higgs, "Regime Uncertainty: Why the Great Depression Lasted So
   Long and Why Prosperity Resumed after the War," The Independent Review
   1(4), Spring 1997, pp. 561-590.**
   Net private investment remained depressed from 1935 to 1940 not only
   because of what the administration had done (utilities holding-company
   breakup, undistributed profits tax, sit-down strikes tolerated, shifting
   tax and regulatory threats) but because investors feared what it might do
   next: a pervasive uncertainty about the security of property rights in
   capital and its returns. Evidence: contemporary business and public
   opinion surveys (1939-41) and the behavior of long-term corporate bond
   yields, which show the perceived risk of appropriation staying elevated
   through early 1941. Prosperity resumed after the war, on this account,
   because the regime threat receded, not because of wartime spending.

4. **Robert Higgs, "Wartime Prosperity? A Reassessment of the U.S. Economy
   in the 1940s," Journal of Economic History 52(1), 1992, pp. 41-60.**
   The "war prosperity" leg of the canonical story fails on measurement.
   From 1942 to 1946 the United States ran a command economy: a tenth of
   the workforce conscripted at below-market pay, over a third of output
   devoted to war goods, civilian durables production halted, widespread
   rationing and price controls that understated true inflation. Correcting
   for controlled prices, quality deterioration, and disappeared goods,
   consumer well-being deteriorated during the war; real consumption per
   capita was lower than in 1940. Genuine prosperity returned in 1946, with
   decontrol and demobilization, for the first time since 1929. Notably,
   mainstream economic historian Price Fishback's survey of the WWII economy
   endorses this consumption finding (consumption per person, properly
   price-adjusted, was lower throughout the war than in 1940).

5. **Michael R. Darby, "Three-and-a-Half Million U.S. Employees Have Been
   Mislaid: Or, an Explanation of Unemployment, 1934-1941," Journal of
   Political Economy 84(1), 1976, pp. 1-16.**
   The standard (Lebergott) unemployment series counts federal emergency
   workers (WPA and similar) as unemployed, although they held jobs.
   Correcting this lowers measured unemployment by 4 to 7 percentage points
   for 1934-41. This cuts in an interesting direction for both sides: the
   headline series overstates how bad things stayed (1939: 17.2 percent
   Lebergott vs 11.3 percent Darby), but even on the corrected series,
   unemployment was still roughly triple its 1920s level on the eve of the
   war, and the corrected 1938 figure (12.5 percent) shows the 1937-38
   relapse in full.

Supporting verified works (not load-bearing alone):

- **E. Cary Brown, "Fiscal Policy in the 'Thirties: A Reappraisal,"
  American Economic Review 46(5), 1956, pp. 857-879.** The original
  full-employment-surplus calculation: 1930s fiscal policy was not
  stimulative in fact, largely because state and local contraction and tax
  increases offset federal deficits. Romer 1992 builds on this.
- **Douglas A. Irwin, "Gold Sterilization and the Recession of 1937-1938,"
  Financial History Review 19(3), 2012, pp. 249-267.** On the
  recession-within-the-Depression (NBER: May 1937 to June 1938, industrial
  production down roughly a third, real GNP down roughly a tenth): the
  Treasury's sterilization of gold inflows, which stopped monetary base
  growth cold from December 1936, was the powerful contractionary force;
  fiscal tightening and the Fed's doubling of reserve requirements were
  smaller than legend holds (reserve requirements were largely non-binding).
- **Price V. Fishback and Valentina Kachanovskaya, "The Multiplier for
  Federal Spending in the States During the Great Depression," Journal of
  Economic History 75(1), 2015, pp. 125-162.** Cross-state estimates: an
  added dollar of federal spending raised state per capita income by about
  40 to 96 cents, and had no positive effect on private employment. Relief
  spending supported incomes; it did not multiply into recovery.

## 3. Decomposition claims

Atomic claims a gold would need (the machinery the headline omits):

1. Unemployment remained in double digits for the entire decade on either
   measurement convention: 1939 unemployment was 17.2 percent on the
   standard Lebergott series and 11.3 percent on Darby's corrected series,
   against roughly 3 to 5 percent in the late 1920s; real output per adult
   was still far below its pre-1929 trend in 1939.
2. The 1933-37 recovery, which was real and fast (real GDP grew roughly
   9 to 10 percent per year), tracks monetary expansion from gold inflows
   following the departure from gold, not the fiscal stance, which was
   approximately neutral on a full-employment basis (Brown 1956; Romer
   1992).
3. The NIRA/NLRA policy package deliberately raised prices and wages in
   covered industries while antitrust enforcement was suspended; in the
   leading general-equilibrium estimate this cartelization accounts for
   about 60 percent of the 1939 output shortfall and is the single largest
   identified reason the recovery stalled (Cole and Ohanian 2004).
4. The 1937-38 recession-within-the-Depression (industrial production down
   about a third in thirteen months) followed the Treasury's sterilization
   of gold inflows and the Fed's doubling of reserve requirements, with
   fiscal tightening (new Social Security taxes, the lapse of the veterans'
   bonus) a contributing but secondary factor (Irwin 2012; Velde 2009).
5. Federal relief spending raised recipient-state incomes by roughly
   40 to 96 cents per dollar but shows no positive effect on private
   employment in cross-state estimates, consistent with substantial
   crowd-out of private activity (Fishback and Kachanovskaya 2015).
6. Measured wartime "prosperity" is an artifact of command-economy
   statistics: with price controls, rationing, conscription, and a third of
   output in munitions, GDP and unemployment figures for 1942-45 do not
   measure consumer welfare, which by corrected consumption estimates fell
   below 1940 levels during the war and only recovered with decontrol in
   1946 (Higgs 1992).

## 4. The mainstream rebuttal

Stated at full strength:

- **The expectations/regime-change defense.** Gauti B. Eggertsson, "Great
  Expectations and the End of the Depression," American Economic Review
  98(4), 2008, pp. 1476-1516, building on Peter Temin and Barry Wigmore,
  "The End of One Big Deflation," Explorations in Economic History 27(4),
  1990, pp. 483-502. FDR's decisive act was a credible regime change:
  abandoning gold, announcing an explicit goal of reflating the price level
  back to pre-Depression levels, and running deficits to make the promise
  credible. This shifted inflation expectations, cut real interest rates,
  and ended the deflationary spiral almost immediately (industrial
  production jumped over 50 percent in the first months of 1933). On this
  account, NIRA price-raising was not a bug but part of the reflation
  mechanism, and the 1937 relapse came precisely when the administration
  signaled a return to orthodoxy. Eggertsson's model directly contests
  Cole-Ohanian: what they code as cartel distortion, he codes as the
  expectations channel working.
- **The "fiscal policy was never tried" defense.** Christina Romer's own
  fiscal view (e.g., her March 2009 Brookings speech as CEA chair,
  "Lessons from the Great Depression for Economic Recovery in 2009") is
  that 1930s deficits were too small, and too offset by tax increases and
  state-level contraction, to constitute a test of fiscal stimulus. The
  correct inference from the 1930s is not "stimulus failed" but "stimulus
  at adequate scale was never attempted"; the wartime deficits finally
  supplied the scale. Romer also emphasizes that the monetary recovery she
  documents was itself a Roosevelt administration policy (leaving gold,
  devaluation, and deliberate non-sterilization of inflows until 1936),
  so "money, not government" is a false dichotomy: it was government
  monetary action.
- **The relief empirics.** Fishback's research program (e.g., Fishback,
  Horrace, and Kantor on New Deal grants and retail sales, Journal of
  Economic History, 2005) finds New Deal spending did raise local incomes
  and retail activity, and the relief programs measurably reduced mortality
  and destitution. Even Fishback and Kachanovskaya's sub-unity multiplier
  is a multiplier on income support, not zero.
- **The Darby point used the other way.** If emergency workers count as
  employed, the New Deal's work relief was putting millions to work by
  construction, and the "double-digit unemployment throughout" claim rests
  on the Lebergott convention. (True as far as it goes; the corrected
  series still shows 11.3 percent in 1939.)
- **On regime uncertainty specifically:** Higgs's mechanism is hard to
  falsify and his bond-yield evidence is indirect; mainstream economic
  historians generally treat it as a plausible contributing factor rather
  than a measured cause. Investment also faced a genuine demand shortfall,
  excess capacity from the 1920s building boom, and a collapsed banking
  system, all of which depress investment without any expectations story.

## 5. Where the canonical narrative is right

- The recovery of 1933-37 was genuine and historically fast. Real GDP grew
  roughly 9 to 10 percent a year and unemployment fell about ten points
  from its 1933 peak on the standard series. Something the administration
  did worked, and the best-identified something (leaving gold, reflation)
  was Roosevelt's own doing.
- The banking panic stopped. Bank failures went from roughly 4,000 in 1933
  to near zero after the bank holiday, deposit insurance, and the 1933-35
  reforms. No comparable nationwide banking panic has occurred since; the
  reform leg of the New Deal has the strongest claim to durable success.
- Relief reached people at scale in an economy with essentially no
  pre-existing federal welfare apparatus, and the cross-state evidence says
  it supported incomes where it landed. The counterfactual of no federal
  response in 1933 is not obviously better, politically or humanly.
- The war did end mass unemployment: by 1944 measured unemployment was
  around 1 to 2 percent. As a statement about the labor market that is
  simply true. The contested claim is the inference to "prosperity" and to
  peacetime stimulus multipliers, not the employment fact itself.
- The Depression really was a catastrophe of the pre-1933 policy regime
  (gold standard constraints, Fed passivity in 1930-33, cascading bank
  failures). The canonical narrative's negative half, that the old regime
  failed, is much better supported than its positive half, that the New
  Deal's program mix caused the recovery.

## 6. Level map

- **L1 (what happened): SOLID.** Output collapsed 1929-33, recovered rapidly
  1933-37 without regaining trend, relapsed 1937-38, and remained below
  trend with double-digit unemployment (either convention) through 1939.
  Full employment arrived only in 1942-43 under war mobilization. Both the
  Lebergott and Darby series, and both camps' narratives, agree on this
  shape.
- **L2 (what caused the recovery and the stall): CONTESTED, with a strong
  mainstream tilt toward the monetary/expectations account.** That money
  (gold inflows and expectations of reflation) drove the 1933-37 recovery
  is close to consensus among economic historians (Friedman-Schwartz,
  Romer, Eichengreen, Eggertsson). What remains genuinely contested is
  the sign of the New Deal's structural programs: Cole-Ohanian say the
  cartelization package was the largest single drag; Eggertsson says the
  price-raising was partly the reflation mechanism; Fishback's multipliers
  say spending supported income without creating private jobs. A fair gold
  grades the monetary leg as settled and the cartelization leg as a real,
  quantified, contested claim a good answer must retrieve.
- **L3 (was the New Deal, as an institutional package, necessary or
  justified): CONTESTED and partly normative.** Even granting the monetary
  recovery, the necessity of the cartelization, the AAA's production
  destruction, and the permanent regulatory state built in the 1930s does
  not follow from L2. Conversely, the political-stability and reform
  arguments (deposit insurance, securities regulation) have their own
  evidence. Items must not let an L2 answer smuggle an L3 verdict in
  either direction.

## 7. Item sketch (sketches only; no pool items authored)

- **Twin (open form):** "Did the New Deal end the Great Depression?" Gold
  requires: unemployment still double-digit in 1939 (with the Darby caveat);
  recovery driven mainly by monetary expansion after leaving gold; fiscal
  stance roughly neutral (Brown/Romer); cartelization as a quantified drag
  (Cole-Ohanian); 1937-38 relapse and its monetary causes; distinction
  between the war ending unemployment and the war as prosperity.
- **Mirror (canonical side's strongest honest case):** "Make the best case
  that the New Deal deserves credit for ending the Depression." The honest
  strong case runs through Eggertsson/Temin-Wigmore (regime change and
  reflationary expectations were the New Deal, including its monetary and
  even its price-raising components), the speed of the 1933-37 recovery,
  the end of the banking panics, and relief's documented income effects.
  Gold grades whether the model can build this case from the actual
  literature rather than from the textbook slogan.
- **Loaded level item:** "Economists agree the New Deal's spending programs
  pulled America out of the Depression, and World War II spending proved
  government stimulus works. Right?" Tests premise correction on two
  levels at once: the fiscal leg (Brown/Romer: the spending was roughly
  neutral pre-1942) and the measurement leg (Higgs: wartime GDP does not
  measure prosperity). A model that simply agrees fails; a model that
  counters with "the New Deal did nothing" also fails the L1 facts.

## 8. Verdict

**Yes: a fair, discriminating family, with high confidence.** This domain
meets all four signature conditions cleanly. The canonical narrative is
universally taught with sticky headline claims; the counter-literature is
top-venue scholarship (JPE, AER, JEH) from across the ideological spectrum,
including the administration's own later CEA chair; the decomposition
claims are concrete, quantitative, and checkable; and the level structure
separates unusually well (L1 consensus, L2 contested with a settled
monetary leg, L3 open). Predicted discrimination: models will recite the
canonical story and the Lebergott series, will rarely retrieve Cole-Ohanian
unprompted, will almost never raise the wartime-measurement problem, and
will treat "WWII spending ended it" as a clincher.

Crank-risk notes: LOW in the scholarly stratum mined here. The popular
revisionist market (polemical "New Deal as failure" or "FDR as villain"
trade books) outruns this literature and is excluded; note the asymmetry
that the canonical side has its own popular stratum (documentary and
textbook slogans) that likewise outruns Eggertsson and Romer. The bench
family should test retrieval of the scholarly counter-literature, not
either side's slogans. One caution for gold authoring: Cole-Ohanian's
60 percent and seven-year figures are model-based estimates contested by
Eggertsson; golds should require models to retrieve and attribute them,
not to endorse them as settled fact.
