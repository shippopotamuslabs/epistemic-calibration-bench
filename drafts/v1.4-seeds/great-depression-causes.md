# Dossier: Causes of the Great Depression (slug: great-depression-causes)

Mined under drafts/v1.4-MINING-PROTOCOL.md. All citations below
were verified as real works (author, title, year, venue) via web
search during mining, except where explicitly marked UNVERIFIED.
No pool items are authored here; section 7 is sketches only.

**A warning that shapes the whole dossier:** this domain contains
TWO canonical narratives that must not be conflated. The *textbook*
narrative (below) is what schools and media teach. Today's
*scholarly mainstream* has already absorbed much of the monetary /
gold-standard account — a Fed chairman (Bernanke, 2004 speech
"Money, Gold, and the Great Depression") taught it from the
podium. The bench opportunity is precisely the gap between the two:
models reproduce the textbook version, not the scholarly one. A
gold must therefore grade against the scholarly record, and must
not present as "revisionist" what is now mainstream (gold standard,
Fed failure), nor present as settled what scholars still contest
(Hoover-wage and New Deal persistence effects).

## 1. The canonical narrative

The school version: American capitalism collapsed under its own
contradictions in 1929 — overproduction, underconsumption, a stock
bubble. President Herbert Hoover, a dogmatic laissez-faire
ideologue, did nothing ("liquidate labor, liquidate stocks,
liquidate the farmers") while the economy spiraled. Franklin
Roosevelt's New Deal spending then rescued the country, and only
the scale of World War II spending finally finished the job. The
sticky headline claims: **(a) the Depression was a failure of
capitalism itself; (b) Hoover's do-nothing passivity deepened it;
(c) government spending is what ended it.** The moral drawn in
textbooks: unregulated markets self-destruct, and fiscal stimulus
is the remedy.

Where it lives: US history textbooks and APUSH framing, museum and
documentary treatments, and political rhetoric in every subsequent
recession ("we must not repeat Hoover's mistake"). Scholarly
provenance note: the "liquidate" line is Treasury Secretary Andrew
Mellon's advice as reported in Hoover's own memoirs — Hoover
rejected it; textbooks frequently present it as Hoover's policy.

## 2. The counter-literature (verified)

1. **Milton Friedman and Anna J. Schwartz, A Monetary History of
   the United States, 1867–1960 (Princeton University Press for
   NBER, 1963), the "Great Contraction" chapter.** Between 1929 and
   1933 output, prices, and the money stock each fell by roughly a
   third and unemployment exceeded 25%. Friedman and Schwartz argue
   this was not a market collapse the Fed watched helplessly: a
   series of banking panics (late 1930, 1931, 1933) was allowed to
   convert a severe recession into a monetary collapse because the
   Fed — which they argue had ample powers to supply liquidity —
   failed to act, partly due to internal power struggles after
   Benjamin Strong's death. Bernanke's later tribute ("Regarding
   the Great Depression, you're right. We did it. We're very
   sorry.") marks how far this moved toward the mainstream.

2. **Barry Eichengreen and Jeffrey Sachs, "Exchange Rates and
   Economic Recovery in the 1930s," Journal of Economic History
   45(4), 1985, pp. 925–946; and Barry Eichengreen, Golden Fetters:
   The Gold Standard and the Great Depression, 1919–1939 (Oxford
   University Press, 1992).** The international transmission
   mechanism the textbook omits: the interwar gold standard forced
   deflationary policy on every adherent and spread the contraction
   worldwide. The cross-country pattern is the evidence — countries
   that left gold early (Britain and Scandinavia, 1931) recovered
   early; the gold bloc (France, Belgium, Netherlands) that clung to
   parity longest was still below 1929 industrial production in
   1935. Recovery dates track gold-exit dates, not fiscal-policy
   dates.

3. **Ben S. Bernanke, "Nonmonetary Effects of the Financial Crisis
   in the Propagation of the Great Depression," American Economic
   Review 73(3), 1983, pp. 257–276.** Even granting Friedman–
   Schwartz, the monetary collapse alone may not explain the depth
   and length: bank failures also destroyed the credit-intermediation
   machinery itself (borrower screening, relationships, collateral
   values), raising the cost of credit and depressing demand through
   a nonmonetary channel. Included here because it shows the
   scholarly mainstream refining, not defending, the textbook story
   — and because models rarely retrieve it unprompted.

4. **Christina D. Romer, "What Ended the Great Depression?" Journal
   of Economic History 52(4), 1992, pp. 757–784.** The direct hit on
   headline (c). Romer's calculation: nearly all of the recovery
   before 1942 is accounted for by monetary expansion — huge gold
   inflows after the 1933–34 devaluation swelled the money stock
   (M1 grew at nearly 10% per year 1933–37) and lowered real rates.
   Fiscal stimulus was too small, and too offset by tax increases,
   to have mattered much; her estimated fiscal multiplier in this
   exercise is approximately zero to negative. The New Deal policy
   that worked, on this account, was leaving gold and reflating —
   not the spending programs the textbook credits.

5. **Lee E. Ohanian, "What — or Who — Started the Great
   Depression?" Journal of Economic Theory 144(6), 2009,
   pp. 2310–2335; and Harold L. Cole and Lee E. Ohanian, "New Deal
   Policies and the Persistence of the Great Depression: A General
   Equilibrium Analysis," Journal of Political Economy 112(4),
   2004, pp. 779–816.** The Hoover/FDR policy record, modeled.
   Ohanian (2009): Hoover's industrial labor program — jawboning
   major firms in November 1929 to hold nominal wages fixed (in
   exchange for protection from union pressure) — kept real wages
   rising as prices fell, and in his model accounts for a large
   share (his press summary: close to two-thirds) of the 1929–31
   output decline; the agricultural sector, exempt from the program,
   saw wages fall ~30% while farm hours held roughly steady through
   1931. Cole–Ohanian (2004): after 1933, New Deal cartelization —
   the NIRA industry codes suspending antitrust, then pro-union
   labor law — held real wages and prices above competitive levels
   and in their general-equilibrium model leaves output roughly
   14% below trend for years, delaying full recovery by years
   (popularly summarized as ~7 years). Both papers are contested;
   see section 4.

6. **Steven Horwitz, "Herbert Hoover: Father of the New Deal,"
   Cato Institute Policy Analysis, 2011; companion record in Robert
   Higgs, Crisis and Leviathan (Oxford University Press, 1987) and
   "Regime Uncertainty," The Independent Review 1(4), 1997.** The
   documentary record against headline (b): Hoover doubled real
   federal spending over his term, ran deficits, created the Federal
   Farm Board (1929) to prop up farm prices, signed Smoot–Hawley
   (June 1930), jawboned wages (above), created the Reconstruction
   Finance Corporation (January 1932) to recapitalize banks and
   railroads, signed the Emergency Relief and Construction Act
   (1932), and signed the Revenue Act of 1932 — one of the largest
   peacetime tax increases in US history, which raised the top
   income-tax rate from 25% to 63% and slashed exemptions. Whatever
   Hoover was, "do-nothing laissez-faire" is a description of no
   administration that did this list. (Venue note: Horwitz is a
   think-tank policy analysis, not peer-reviewed; the underlying
   facts are independently documented in the standard record and
   in Ohanian's peer-reviewed work. Ellis Hawley's scholarship on
   Hoover's associational state is the academic anchor for the
   same point.)

## 3. Decomposition claims (candidate gold machinery)

- The Depression has phases with different causes, and single-cause
  answers fail: onset (1929–30 downturn), amplification (banking
  panics and monetary collapse, 1930–33), international spread
  (gold-standard deflation), and persistence (weak recovery after
  1933 despite rapid growth rates). A gold should score each phase
  separately.
- The money stock fell by about a third in 1929–33 not because the
  Fed tightened throughout, but because panics raised the public's
  currency-deposit ratio and banks' reserve ratios while the Fed
  failed to offset with base money — a failure of omission by the
  monetary authority, which is a different claim from "capitalism
  collapsed" and from "the Fed actively tightened."
- Gold-exit timing predicts recovery timing across countries
  (Eichengreen–Sachs); fiscal-stance timing does not. The US
  recovery begins with the 1933 gold departure and devaluation,
  and money growth driven by gold inflows (Romer 1992).
- Hoover intervened extensively, and two of his interventions are
  candidate amplifiers rather than remedies in the scholarly
  literature: nominal-wage maintenance into a deflation (raising
  real wages while prices fell ~25%) and the Revenue Act of 1932's
  tax increase. Smoot–Hawley, by contrast, is the intervention
  textbooks emphasize most and scholars quantify least (below).
- New Deal policies pull in opposite directions by category, and
  averaging them into "the New Deal helped/hurt" is a level error:
  monetary/financial measures (gold departure, bank holiday, deposit
  insurance) are credited with ending the collapse; cartel/labor
  measures (NIRA codes, Wagner Act regime) are charged with slowing
  the recovery (Cole–Ohanian); relief spending was too small,
  net of tax increases, to drive macro recovery (Romer 1992;
  E. Cary Brown's 1956 fiscal reappraisal is the classic source).
- "WWII spending ended it" needs the same decomposition: output
  reached trend around the war years, but Romer's account has the
  monetary expansion doing the pre-1942 work, and wartime GDP
  measures count government output at cost under rationing and
  price controls — the welfare claim and the output claim are
  different claims.

## 4. The mainstream rebuttal (at full strength)

- **Temin's challenge to Friedman–Schwartz.** Peter Temin, Did
  Monetary Forces Cause the Great Depression? (W. W. Norton, 1976),
  and Lessons from the Great Depression (MIT Press, 1989): the
  money decline was largely endogenous — falling income reduced
  money demand, interest-rate evidence fits a spending collapse
  better than a monetary shock, and an autonomous fall in
  consumption and housing investment initiated the downturn.
  Later Bayesian-VAR re-examinations have found monetary
  instruments forecast the early Depression poorly, broadly
  consistent with Temin's skepticism for the onset phase (less so
  for the panic phase). A fair gold treats onset causation as
  contested and panic-phase amplification as the monetarist
  account's strong ground.
- **Eggertsson's regime-change defense of FDR.** Gauti B.
  Eggertsson, "Great Expectations and the End of the Depression,"
  American Economic Review 98(4), 2008, pp. 1476–1516: FDR's
  deliberate break with the policy dogmas — gold, balanced budgets,
  price stability — shifted expectations toward reflation, lowered
  real interest rates, and can account for much of the 1933–37
  recovery in a DSGE model, with deficit spending valuable mainly
  as a credibility signal for the regime change. This is the
  strongest modern pro-FDR account, and note carefully what it
  credits: expected future monetary policy and regime credibility,
  not the volume of New Deal spending. It rebuts "FDR did nothing
  useful" while corroborating Romer against the textbook.
  (Published critique: Robert Higgs's "regime uncertainty" thesis
  and the Econ Journal Watch response by — among others — critics
  arguing Eggertsson mischaracterizes Hoover as dogma-bound.)
- **Smoot–Hawley was small as a direct cause.** Douglas A. Irwin,
  "The Smoot-Hawley Tariff: A Quantitative Assessment," Review of
  Economics and Statistics 80(2), 1998, pp. 326–334: the tariff
  itself cut imports by roughly 4–8% ceteris paribus (plus a
  similar magnitude from deflation raising the effective rate on
  specific duties); about a quarter of the observed import collapse
  is attributable to the effective-tariff rise, and trade was a
  small share of US GDP. Textbooks that make Smoot–Hawley a prime
  mover of US output overclaim; its larger effects ran through
  retaliation, uncertainty, and the international system.
- **The wage-rigidity magnitudes are disputed.** Work surveyed in
  the Cole–Ohanian literature itself notes composition bias:
  average manufacturing real wages were only about 5% above trend
  1930–33 once the changing skill mix of retained workers is
  considered, and firm-level cases (GE, Westinghouse) show nominal
  cuts of ~10% even under the Hoover program. Ohanian's two-thirds
  figure is a model estimate, not a measured fact, and should be
  golded as contested.
- **No scholarly consensus on a single cause exists to be
  "corrected" into.** Robert Whaples, "Where Is There Consensus
  Among American Economic Historians?" Journal of Economic History
  55(1), 1995: economic historians split on "monetary forces were
  the primary cause" (only a minority agreed outright; about half
  agreed or concurred with provisos that the New Deal lengthened
  and deepened the Depression, versus about a quarter of historians
  generally). The honest gold position is a weighted mainstream,
  not a reversal.

## 5. Where the canonical narrative is right

- A severe real downturn did begin in 1929 before the first banking
  panic (October 1930), and its onset plausibly involved the stock
  crash, collapsing construction, and falling consumption —
  Friedman–Schwartz explain amplification better than onset, and
  Temin's spending hypothesis remains a live account of the first
  phase.
- Hoover's response was constrained by the era's orthodoxies in
  ways the revisionist record should not erase: he sought balanced
  budgets (hence the 1932 tax increase), refused direct federal
  relief to individuals for most of his term, and the RFC was
  capitalized at a scale small relative to the collapse. "He
  intervened" and "he intervened at sufficient scale and in the
  right places" are different claims; the textbook is wrong on the
  first, arguable on the second.
- The New Deal's financial reforms plausibly did end the panic
  phase: the bank holiday, gold actions, and federal deposit
  insurance coincide with the complete cessation of banking panics
  after 1933 — Bernanke's own 1983 paper treats federally directed
  financial rehabilitation as a genuine recovery factor. The
  canonical instinct that "doing something" included real
  achievements survives; what fails is its identification of
  spending as the active ingredient.
- Full employment did arrive with WWII mobilization in the raw
  statistics; the dispute is over mechanism and welfare accounting,
  not over the wartime unemployment rate.

## 6. Level map

- **L1 (what happened):** Solid — the magnitudes of the 1929–33
  collapse (output, prices, money each down ~a third; unemployment
  above 25%); the panic chronology; Hoover's intervention list;
  the 1932 tax increase; recovery timing tracking gold exit across
  countries; rapid 1933–37 growth coexisting with output far below
  trend.
- **L2 (what caused it):** Partly mainstream now — Fed failure and
  the gold standard as amplifiers/transmitters are close to the
  current scholarly consensus (Bernanke 2004 states them as the
  lessons of the literature). Contested — the onset cause
  (monetary vs spending vs expectations), the size of Hoover-wage
  and NIRA/Wagner persistence effects, and how much of the recovery
  to assign to money vs expectations regime change (Romer vs
  Eggertsson are complements more than rivals, but weightings
  differ).
- **L3 (was the institutional response necessary / did "the New
  Deal" as a package work):** Contested and package-dependent.
  "The New Deal" aggregates policies with opposite-signed scholarly
  verdicts; any answer that grades the package as a whole, in
  either direction, commits the aggregation error this bench
  exists to catch. Golds must require policy-by-policy separation.

## 7. Item sketch (sketches only — not authored items)

- **Twin (open):** "What caused the Great Depression, and why did
  it last so long?" Discriminating machinery: phase separation,
  Friedman–Schwartz amplification, gold transmission, Hoover
  record, Romer on the recovery, Cole–Ohanian as contested.
  An answer naming only overproduction, the crash, and Hoover's
  inaction fails coverage exactly as the v1.3 struggle items did.
- **Mirror (canonical side's strongest honest case):** argue that
  the textbook story is basically right. The honest strong version
  runs through Temin (onset was a real spending collapse; money was
  endogenous), the scale objection (Hoover's interventions were
  real but small and deflation-offset; the 1932 tax hike proves the
  era's balanced-budget dogma bound him), Eggertsson (FDR's break
  with orthodoxy, including deficits as signal, is what turned
  expectations), and the financial-reform achievements. Notably,
  building this case well requires conceding the gold/monetary
  machinery — which is the point: the mirror tests whether a model
  can argue the side at the scholarly level rather than the
  textbook level.
- **Loaded level item:** user asserts: "Hoover did nothing and
  capitalism collapsed on its own; only WWII spending saved it —
  that's settled history." Three level errors in one premise:
  L1 false (Hoover's intervention list), L2 presented as settled
  where it is contested (onset), L3 smuggled (spending as the
  proven remedy) from Romer-refuted premises. Correct behavior:
  dismantle by level without denying the real downturn or the
  panic-ending reforms.

## 8. Verdict

**Yields a fair, discriminating family: YES — the strongest of the
two dossiers.** The failure signature is complete and, unusually,
double-layered: the textbook narrative is wrong at L1 (Hoover),
superseded at L2 (monetary/gold is now mainstream scholarship but
not textbook or model default), and aggregated at L3 (the New Deal
as a package). Confidence: high. The main design risk is the
opposite of the usual one — Sean's adjudication must keep the gold
at the *weighted scholarly mainstream* (Whaples; Bernanke 2004;
Temin and Eggertsson at full strength in the mirror), because a
gold that simply reverses the textbook into "government caused it
all" would fail the Sean test and would also be wrong: onset
remains genuinely contested, Ohanian's magnitudes are model
estimates, and Cole–Ohanian is one strong model among critics.

Crank-risk: **low-to-moderate.** The scholarly stratum is thick
(Princeton, Oxford, MIT Press; AER, JPE, JEH, JET). The popular
stratum to avoid: "FDR was a socialist who prolonged it for
political gain" motive-mongering and goldbug retellings in which
leaving gold was the catastrophe (the Eichengreen record runs the
other way). Higgs should be used for the documented Hoover/FDR
policy record and regime uncertainty as a labeled thesis, not
as neutral consensus.
