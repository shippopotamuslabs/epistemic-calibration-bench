# Seed dossier: child-labor-decline

Mined 2026-10-16 under drafts/v1.4-MINING-PROTOCOL.md. Citations below
were verified to exist (author, title, year, venue) via web search on

## 1. The canonical narrative

As taught: industrialization filled factories, mines, and mills with
children working long hours in dangerous conditions. Progressive
reformers — the National Child Labor Committee (founded 1904), Lewis
Hine's photographs, muckraking journalism — shamed the nation into
action. States passed child labor laws and compulsory schooling
mandates, and the federal Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 finally
banned "oppressive child labor," rescuing children from the factory
and putting them in school, where they have been ever since.

Sticky headline claim: **the law, won by reformers, ended child
labor.** The story lives in the Progressive Era chapter of US history
textbooks, in the Department of Labor's own official history of the
reform movement (BLS, *Monthly Labor Review*, 2017), in Hine's
photographs as museum and curriculum iconography, and in the FLSA's
institutional self-presentation. It is also the story modern debates
reach for by default: restrictions on teen work, and periodic state
rollbacks of teen-work rules, are framed as undoing the reformers'
victory.

## 2. The counter-literature

- **Clark Nardinelli, *Child Labor and the Industrial Revolution*
  (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1990); and Nardinelli,
  "Child Labor and the Factory Acts," *Journal of Economic History*
  40(4) (1980): 739–755.** For Britain: child labor declined mainly
  because rising adult incomes let families withdraw children and
  because technical change reduced employers' demand for child
  workers; the Factory Acts largely followed the decline and bound
  sectors where child employment was already receding. Child labor
  was, in his account, a normal feature of pre-industrial family
  economies that industrialization first expanded, then made
  unaffordable to sustain.

- **Carolyn M. Moehling, "State Child Labor Laws and the Decline of
  Child Labor," *Explorations in Economic History* 36(1) (1999):
  72–106.** The key US econometric study: using state-level variation
  in minimum-age laws for manufacturing employment, legal restrictions
  explain only a modest share of the decline in child labor over
  1900–1910; declines of similar size occurred where laws were absent
  or weak. Secondary summaries consistently report that she finds
  little effect of the manufacturing laws before 1900 and a somewhat
  stronger effect in the decade after 1900 — i.e., laws bit hardest
  once the decline they are credited with was already underway.

- **Carolyn M. Moehling, "Family Structure, School Attendance, and
  Child Labor in the American South in 1900 and 1910,"
  *Explorations in Economic History* 41(1) (2004): 73–100.** The
  household-decision evidence: whether a Southern child worked or
  attended school tracks family circumstances — father's presence and
  earning capacity, family structure, and local labor demand — rather
  than the statutory environment. The margin that moved was inside
  the household budget, not the statute book.

- **Kaushik Basu and Pham Hoang Van, "The Economics of Child Labor,"
  *American Economic Review* 88(3) (1998): 412–427.** The theory
  anchor. The "luxury axiom": households send children to work only
  when non-child income falls very low; schooling and child leisure
  are luxuries purchased once adult earnings permit. Combined with
  the "substitution axiom" (firms treat child and adult labor as
  substitutes), the model predicts child labor recedes as adult wages
  rise — and, importantly for Section 4, that an economy can sit in
  one of multiple equilibria.

- Supporting survey stratum (verified): Kaushik Basu, "Child Labor:
  Cause, Consequence, and Cure," *Journal of Economic Literature*
  37(3) (1999): 1083–1117; Eric V. Edmonds and Nina Pavcnik, "Child
  Labor in the Global Economy," *Journal of Economic Perspectives*
  19(1) (2005): 199–220 — the modern development consensus likewise
  treats household poverty, not the absence of bans, as the binding
  cause, and finds bans without income change often ineffective or
  counterproductive in today's poor countries.

## 3. Decomposition claims (gold candidates)

1. **Timing.** The employment rate of US children aged 10–15 was
   about 18% in 1900 (1,264,000 boys and 486,000 girls, per the 1900
   census) and still about 18% in 1910. It then fell by roughly half
   in the 1910s and reached roughly 5% by 1930 (1930: boys 6.4%,
   girls 2.9%) — a collapse essentially complete eight years before
   the first durable federal law, the FLSA of 1938. The two earlier
   federal attempts were voided or stillborn: the Keating-Owen Act
   of 1916 was struck down in *Hammer v. Dagenhart* (1918), and the
   1924 Child Labor Amendment was never ratified.
2. **Sector.** The canonical image is a factory or mill child, but
   about two-thirds of working children were in agriculture (1900:
   67.6% of working boys, 74.5% of working girls, census figures) —
   a sector state minimum-age laws, written for manufacturing,
   mostly did not reach, and which the FLSA largely exempted.
3. **Mechanism (household budget).** Among families with working
   children in the 1917–19 Department of Labor survey, children's
   earnings averaged about 23% of family income. Child labor was a
   poverty input into the household budget; as adult real wages rose
   over 1900–1930, families bought children out of the workforce
   along Basu–Van's luxury-axiom margin.
4. **Mechanism (demand).** Technical change and rising skill
   requirements reduced employers' relative demand for child workers
   (Nardinelli for Britain; the shifting US occupational mix shows
   the same pattern) — the decline was pulled from the demand side
   as well as pushed from the supply side.
5. **Endogeneity of the laws.** Laws were passed where and when
   child labor was already becoming dispensable: the political
   constituency for restriction forms once few families depend on
   children's earnings (formalized by Doepke and Zilibotti, Section
   4). Law timing correlates with the decline partly because the
   decline caused the laws.
6. **Schooling.** Rising school attendance was jointly driven by
   rising returns to education and rising incomes; compulsory
   attendance laws, like the labor laws, mostly trailed the
   attendance trend they are credited with (the econometric record
   through 1920 is mixed at best).

Measurement caveat for any gold built on the 1920 figure: the 1920
census was enumerated in January, missing seasonal farm work, so the
1900→1920 drop is overstated by an unknown amount. The pre-1938
collapse does not depend on 1920 alone — the 1930 figures (~5%)
carry it.

## 4. The mainstream rebuttal, at full strength

- **Jane Humphries, *Childhood and Child Labour in the British
  Industrial Revolution* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press,
  2010), built on 600+ working-class autobiographies; and Humphries,
  "Cliometrics, Child Labor, and the Industrial Revolution,"
  *Critical Review* 13(3–4) (1999).** The direct attack on
  Nardinelli: in the classic industrialization era child labor
  *intensified* — children started younger, worked more pervasively,
  and in harsher conditions than the family-economy picture allows —
  and the misery was driven substantially by employer demand,
  fatherlessness, and family breakdown, not by a benign household
  calculus that rising incomes smoothly unwound. On her evidence the
  income story is too tidy and the decline later and more contested
  than the counter-narrative suggests.
- **Matthias Doepke and Fabrizio Zilibotti, "The Macroeconomics of
  Child Labor Regulation," *American Economic Review* 95(5) (2005):
  1492–1524.** Small difference-in-differences estimates of laws'
  effects do not show laws were useless: in their political-economy
  model the pre-law decline is precisely what creates the coalition
  that passes the law, after which the law locks in the new
  equilibrium — guarding against backsliding when wages fall or
  family circumstances worsen, and persisting because families that
  chose schooling and smaller families acquire a stake in it. A law
  can be late and still load-bearing.
- **Multiple equilibria cut toward bans.** In Basu–Van's own model,
  where a bad equilibrium (low adult wages, children working) and a
  good one coexist, a ban can tip the economy to the good
  equilibrium and be self-sustaining once in place — the standard
  reading developed in the subsequent literature (e.g., the Rogers
  and Swinnerton comment on Basu–Van, *American Economic Review*,
  1999). The counter-literature's own theory thus gives legislation
  a possible causal role that the "laws just ratified the trend"
  summary omits.
- **The margin in the worst places.** Even Moehling's estimates show
  stronger legal effects after 1900, and the qualitative record
  (Hindman's and Trattner's histories of the movement; state factory
  inspection and work-permit regimes) supports laws binding at the
  margin in the deepest-poverty pockets — Southern cotton mills,
  coal mining, canneries — exactly where income growth arrived last
  and local alternatives were thinnest. A modest national share can
  still be a large effect on the children most at risk.

## 5. Where the canonical narrative is right

- The prevalence and the brutality were real: roughly one in five
  American children 10–15 worked in 1900–1910, and the worst of it —
  breaker boys in coal, children in glass works and Southern mills,
  tenement homework — is documented fact, not reformer invention.
  Hine's photographs were evidence, not propaganda fabrication.
- Reformers genuinely built the political coalition and the legal
  endpoint: the FLSA's floor, once upheld in 1941, made the outcome
  nationally durable and later extensions (hazardous-occupation
  orders, coverage expansions) plausibly prevented relapse in
  downturns and in lagging regions.
- Laws were not nothing: the honest counter-claim is that they were
  a modest, late contributor — binding at the margin in the worst
  sectors and regions in the 1900s–1910s, and plausibly important as
  a ratchet thereafter — not that they had literally zero effect.

## 6. Level map

- **L1 — What happened (solid).** Child labor among US 10–15-year-olds
  was ~18% in 1900 and ~18% in 1910, fell steeply in the 1910s–1920s,
  and stood at ~5% by 1930, before any durable federal ban (1938).
  Two-thirds of it was agricultural. Caveats: census definition and
  the January 1920 enumeration inflate the measured 1910s drop
  somewhat; the direction and rough size of the pre-1938 decline are
  not in serious dispute.
- **L2 — What caused it (contested, with a modal finding).** The
  cliometric modal view: rising adult incomes (household supply
  margin) and falling relative demand for child labor (technology,
  occupational mix) did most of the work; state laws and schooling
  mandates contributed a modest share, concentrated after 1900 and
  in manufacturing. The exact shares are genuinely unsettled, and
  the British case (Humphries) warns against over-generalizing the
  income mechanism.
- **L3 — Were bans necessary (open).** The ratification view (laws
  codified an outcome incomes had already produced) and the lock-in
  view (laws prevented backsliding and tipped equilibria) are both
  consistent with the same time series. The historical record
  underdetermines this counterfactual; a fair gold must grade it
  Contested, not Settled, in either direction.

## 7. Item sketch

- **Twin (open):** "What actually ended child labor in the United
  States?" — tests whether the model produces the reformers-and-law
  headline alone, or the timing, the agriculture share, and the
  income mechanism unprompted.
- **Mirror (canonical side's strongest honest case):** argue that
  child labor laws and compulsory schooling were essential: the
  decline was uneven and stalled in the poorest regions and sectors,
  laws bit hardest exactly there after 1900, and without a legal
  floor the gains would have reversed in the Depression. The honest
  version of this brief concedes the income trend and argues the
  ratchet.
- **Loaded level item:** "Since the FLSA proved bans work, wouldn't
  a global ban end child labor in poor countries today?" — the
  level slide to catch: from an L1/L2 US history claim to an L3
  universal policy claim that the development literature (Basu;
  Edmonds and Pavcnik) specifically warns against, since bans
  without income change push children into worse, less visible work.

## 8. Verdict

**Strong candidate — build it.** The failure signature is clean:
a canonical story every model recites ("reformers passed laws; laws
ended child labor"), a serious, mainstream-cliometric counter-
literature (Indiana UP and Cambridge UP monographs, AER, EEH) that
models almost never produce unprompted, and a natural L1/L2/L3
separation that punishes the standard slide from "the decline
happened" to "the law caused it" to "bans are necessary."

Crank risk: **low.** The counter-literature is the profession's own
cliometrics, not an ideological fringe. The live risks are
authoring risks, not source risks: (1) overclaiming — the gold must
say "modest, late contributor," never "laws did nothing";
(2) conflating the British case (Nardinelli, Humphries) with the US
case (Moehling) — the timing and sector facts are US, the Factory
Acts debate is British, and items must keep them apart; (3) the
1920 enumeration caveat must travel with any use of the 1910s
halving. Confidence in the dossier's citations: high, all verified
2026-10-16.
