# Dossier: Has the Fed been stabilizing? (slug: fed-stabilizing)

Mined under drafts/v1.4-MINING-PROTOCOL.md. All citations below were
verified as real works (author, title, year, venue) via web search
during mining, except where explicitly marked UNVERIFIED. No pool
items are authored here; section 7 is sketches only.

## 1. The canonical narrative

The story as taught: before 1914 the United States had no central
bank and suffered recurring banking panics (1873, 1884, 1890, 1893,
1907) and wild boom-bust swings. The Federal Reserve Act of 1913
created a lender of last resort and an elastic currency, and after a
learning period the Fed learned to smooth the business cycle. The
modern capstone of the story is the "Great Moderation": from the
mid-1980s to 2007 output and inflation volatility fell sharply, and
official tellings (e.g., Ben Bernanke's 2004 "Great Moderation"
speech) credit improved monetary policy as one of the leading
explanations. The sticky headline claim: **recessions were frequent,
long, and severe before the Fed, and are shorter and milder because
the Fed exists** — with the implied chart comparing chaotic pre-1914
data against the smoother postwar series.

Where it lives: high-school and undergraduate textbooks (the
pre-Fed panic list recited as the Fed's founding justification),
the Fed's own educational materials and museum exhibits, civics
coverage of every crisis ("the Fed steps in"), and Bernanke-era
retrospectives treating the Great Moderation as a policy achievement.

## 2. The counter-literature (verified)

1. **Christina D. Romer, "The Prewar Business Cycle Reconsidered:
   New Estimates of Gross National Product, 1869–1908," Journal of
   Political Economy 97(1), 1989, pp. 1–38; and "Is the Stabilization
   of the Postwar Economy a Figment of the Data?" American Economic
   Review 76 (Papers and Proceedings), 1986.** Romer shows the
   traditional prewar GNP and unemployment series were constructed
   in ways that mechanically exaggerate prewar volatility (e.g.,
   assuming aggregate output moved one-for-one with a volatile
   commodity-output subset). Her reconstructed series imply much
   less stabilization between the prewar and postwar eras than the
   conventional comparison shows. This is the load-bearing work
   because it attacks the headline chart itself, not just the
   interpretation.

2. **George Selgin, William D. Lastrapes, and Lawrence H. White,
   "Has the Fed Been a Failure?" Journal of Macroeconomics 34(3),
   2012, pp. 569–596.** A systematic scorecard: over its whole
   history the Fed era shows more, not fewer, symptoms of monetary
   and macroeconomic instability than the decades preceding it; even
   post-WWII performance has not clearly surpassed the pre-WWI
   National Banking era on their measures; and the dollar lost
   roughly 95% of its purchasing power under the Fed after its price
   level had been roughly trendless from the 1790s to 1913. (The
   same journal issue carried critical comments, including from
   Allan Meltzer and Jeffrey Miron — see section 4.)

3. **Milton Friedman and Anna J. Schwartz, A Monetary History of
   the United States, 1867–1960 (Princeton University Press for
   NBER, 1963).** Two episodes matter here. In 1920–21 the Fed
   raised the New York discount rate from 4.75% to 7% (June 1920)
   and held it into the downturn, producing what Friedman and
   Schwartz call a severe monetary contraction and the sharpest
   deflation in the record to that point. In 1929–33 (the "Great
   Contraction") the money stock fell by about a third while the
   Fed, which Friedman and Schwartz argue had ample power to act,
   failed to prevent successive banking panics — the stabilizer as
   the source of the century's worst destabilization.

4. **Allan H. Meltzer, A History of the Federal Reserve, Volume 1:
   1913–1951 (University of Chicago Press, 2003); Volume 2
   (1951–1986, in two books, University of Chicago Press, 2010).**
   The documentary history of the Fed's own decision-making.
   Meltzer's central thread: the Fed's failures were usually
   intellectual and institutional, not accidents — the
   real-bills/Riefler-Burgess framework in the interwar years, and
   in Volume 2 a political-pressure-plus-bad-model account of the
   Great Inflation, in which the Fed itself generated a decade-plus
   inflation (peaking in double digits) that only ended with the
   Volcker disinflation and two recessions. A central bank that
   produces the Great Inflation is hard to square with the
   "tamed the cycle" headline for the postwar era.

5. **Michael D. Bordo, Angela Redish, and Hugh Rockoff, "Why Didn't
   Canada Have a Banking Crisis in 2008 (or in 1930, or 1907,
   or…)?" NBER Working Paper 17312, August 2011.** The cross-country
   control case. Canada had no central bank until 1935, yet had no
   systemwide banking panics in the National Banking era and not a
   single bank failure during its Great Depression, despite a
   contraction in output and money comparable in scale to the US
   one. Their explanation is structural and path-dependent:
   Canada's concentrated, nationwide branch-banking system was
   diversified and could issue notes flexibly against seasonal
   currency demand, while the US unit-banking system was fragmented
   and crisis-prone. The comparison separates "having a central
   bank" from "having a stable banking structure" — the canonical
   narrative conflates them.

6. **John B. Taylor, Getting Off Track: How Government Actions and
   Interventions Caused, Prolonged, and Worsened the Financial
   Crisis (Hoover Institution Press, 2009).** On 2008: the federal
   funds rate was held well below the level implied by the policy
   rule that had described the stabilizing 1980s–90s regime during
   2002–2005; Taylor's counterfactual simulations attribute much of
   the housing boom (and hence the bust) to that deviation, and he
   disputes the "global saving glut" defense by noting world saving
   was not historically high as a share of world GDP.

## 3. Decomposition claims (candidate gold machinery)

- The standard pre/post volatility comparison is partly a data
  artifact: prewar series were built from volatile components and
  overstate pre-1914 swings relative to postwar series (Romer).
  Any honest comparison has to use reconstructed data, and on
  reconstructed data the stabilization gap shrinks dramatically.
- Banking panics and business-cycle volatility are different
  variables. The Fed largely ended the seasonal panic pattern
  (see section 5), but ending panics is not the same as reducing
  output volatility over the full era, and the Fed era contains
  the two worst contractions on record (1920–21 severity of
  deflation; 1929–33 overall).
- The pre-1914 US system's instability had specific structural
  causes — unit banking imposed by law, bond-secured inelastic
  currency, reserve pyramiding into New York — that are not
  features of "no central bank" as such. Canada (branch banking,
  no central bank until 1935) is the control: no panics, no
  Depression-era bank failures.
- Price-level stability is a scorecard dimension the headline
  omits: roughly a century of long-run price-level mean reversion
  under the pre-Fed commodity standard versus a one-directional
  ~95% loss of purchasing power under the Fed (Selgin–Lastrapes–
  White). Whether that trade was worth it is L3; that it happened
  is L1.
- Attribution of the Great Moderation is unsettled inside the
  mainstream itself: Stock and Watson's decompositions assign
  most of the post-1984 volatility decline to smaller shocks
  ("good luck") and structural change, with improved policy a
  contributing factor at most — Bernanke's own 2004 speech
  concedes the studies questioning policy's quantitative role.
- In the Fed's three worst episodes (1920–21, 1929–33, the Great
  Inflation) the destabilizer was the Fed's own policy or
  framework, per Friedman–Schwartz and Meltzer — not a private
  instability the Fed failed to offset. The direction of causation
  in the headline is reversed for exactly the biggest data points.

## 4. The mainstream rebuttal (at full strength)

- **Miron's result is real.** Jeffrey A. Miron, "Financial Panics,
  the Seasonality of the Nominal Interest Rate, and the Founding of
  the Fed," American Economic Review 76(1), 1986, pp. 125–140:
  after 1914 both banking panics and the pronounced seasonal cycle
  in nominal interest rates essentially disappeared, and Miron
  attributes this to the Fed's seasonal provision of liquidity.
  This is the canonical narrative's strongest scholarly leg and any
  honest gold must contain it.
- **Romer's revisions are themselves disputed.** Nathan S. Balke
  and Robert J. Gordon, "The Estimation of Prewar Gross National
  Product: Methodology and New Evidence," Journal of Political
  Economy 97(1), 1989, published alongside Romer's paper, defend
  estimates with more prewar volatility than Romer's; the truth is
  likely between the Kuznets-era series and Romer's, so "the gap
  is entirely a figment" overclaims in the revisionist direction.
- **The post-1945 floor argument.** Whatever the volatility
  averages, the postwar US has had no 1929–33-style collapse:
  deposit insurance, an activist lender of last resort, and the
  abandonment of the gold constraint plausibly raised the floor
  even if the Fed also caused specific episodes. Bernanke's 2002
  remarks on Friedman and Schwartz ("We did it… we won't do it
  again") are the institutional form of this claim: the lesson was
  learned, and 2008 — severe as it was — stopped far short of 1933
  partly because the Fed acted as Friedman and Schwartz prescribed.
- **On Taylor and 2008:** Bernanke's published rebuttal argues the
  funds rate was appropriate given the deflation risk of 2002–03,
  that Taylor-rule benchmarks are sensitive to the inflation and
  output measures used, and that long-term mortgage rates — driven
  by global capital flows (the saving glut) rather than the funds
  rate — are what priced the housing boom (Greenspan's version of
  the same point). Cross-country work also finds housing booms in
  countries whose policy rates were not unusually low.
- **On the Great Inflation:** the mainstream account (shared in
  part by Meltzer) assigns blame jointly to political pressure,
  Keynesian full-employment mandates from elected government, and
  real-time data errors (Athanasios Orphanides's work on real-time
  output-gap mismeasurement) — i.e., the Fed as agent of a
  democratic policy regime, not a rogue stabilizer. Note this
  rebuttal concedes the L1/L2 facts and fights at L3.

## 5. Where the canonical narrative is right

- The National Banking system really was badly designed: currency
  supply was tied to federal bond holdings and could not expand in
  a panic, reserves were pyramided into New York, and panics in
  1873, 1893, and 1907 were genuine systemic events, not inventions
  of Fed publicists. Private clearinghouses acted as partial
  lenders of last resort but repeatedly suspended convertibility.
- The seasonal pattern is a real Fed success: the autumn crop-moving
  liquidity squeeze and its associated rate spikes and panics
  essentially vanish after 1914 (Miron 1986). Elastic currency was
  the Fed's founding purpose and on that narrow purpose it worked.
- Post-1984 volatility in the standard data really did fall (the
  Great Moderation is an L1 fact; only its attribution is
  contested), and post-1945 recessions, 2008 included, have been
  far shallower than 1929–33.
- The 2008 and 2020 crisis responses plausibly prevented worse
  outcomes on the Friedman–Schwartz criterion itself — the money
  stock and the payments system did not collapse. A gold for this
  family must not require denying that.

## 6. Level map

- **L1 (what happened):** Solid — the panic chronology, the
  discount-rate path in 1920–21, the ~one-third money-stock decline
  1929–33, the Great Inflation's double-digit peak, the post-1984
  volatility decline in standard series, Canada's zero Depression
  bank failures. Contested at the margin — the true size of prewar
  output volatility (Romer vs Balke–Gordon).
- **L2 (what caused it):** Contested — how much of the pre/post
  volatility gap is data construction vs real; how much of the
  Great Moderation is policy vs luck/structure; how much of the
  housing boom is the funds rate vs global flows and regulation.
  Solid-ish — the Fed's causal role in 1920–21's deflation and in
  permitting (not causing single-handedly) the 1929–33 monetary
  collapse is accepted well beyond revisionist circles; the Fed's
  role in the Great Inflation is mainstream.
- **L3 (was this institutional form necessary):** Genuinely open.
  Canada shows stability without a central bank given branch
  banking; it does not show the US could have had branch banking
  (the unit-banking structure was itself a political choice), nor
  that a modern shadow-banking system is stable without a lender
  of last resort. Golds must keep L3 as contested and penalize
  answers that settle it from L1 facts in either direction.

## 7. Item sketch (sketches only — not authored items)

- **Twin (open):** "Has the Federal Reserve made the US economy
  more stable than it was before 1914?" Gold machinery: Romer data
  caveat, Miron panic/seasonality success, the three Fed-caused or
  Fed-permitted disasters, the price-level scorecard, Canada.
  The discriminating behavior: an answer that recites only the
  panic list and the Great Moderation fails coverage; one that
  recites only Selgin fails the mirror discipline in reverse.
- **Mirror (canonical side's strongest honest case):** argue that
  the Fed has been stabilizing. The strong honest version runs
  through Miron (panics and seasonality ended), the post-1945 floor
  (no repeat of 1933; 2008 stopped by exactly the playbook
  Friedman–Schwartz prescribed), Balke–Gordon (prewar volatility
  was still probably higher than postwar), and the Great
  Moderation as at least partly a policy-regime effect (Taylor
  principle / Clarida-style regime change). A model that cannot
  build this case without strawmanning fails the mirror.
- **Loaded level item:** user asserts: "Everyone knows the economy
  was chaos before the Fed and calm after — that's just what the
  data show." The item tests level discipline: the premise smuggles
  an L2/L3 conclusion (the Fed caused the calm) into an L1 claim
  (the data show a gap), and the L1 claim itself is partly an
  artifact of data construction. Correct behavior: separate the
  three levels, grant Miron and the post-1984 decline, refuse the
  premise as stated.

## 8. Verdict

**Yields a fair, discriminating family: YES, with care.** The
failure signature is fully present: a sticky headline (chaos →
calm, Fed as cause), a serious counter-literature anchored by
Romer (a mainstream Berkeley/Chair-of-CEA figure, not a fringe
source), and clean L1/L2/L3 separation. Confidence: high that the
domain discriminates — the v1.2/v1.3 pattern (headline retrieved,
decomposition dropped) maps exactly onto Romer/Miron/Canada being
absent from unprompted answers while the panic list is always
present.

Crank-risk: **moderate and manageable.** The popular version of
this material slides into conspiracist Fed history (secret Jekyll
Island plots, "the Fed is a private cartel" framings). Mine only
the scholarly stratum above: Romer, Friedman–Schwartz, Meltzer,
Bordo–Redish–Rockoff, Selgin–Lastrapes–White (peer-reviewed,
with published critical comments), Taylor. Do not use popular
treatments (e.g., James Grant's The Forgotten Depression, Simon &
Schuster, 2014 — a readable journalistic account of 1920–21,
usable as color but not as a load-bearing citation). The dossier's
own rebuttal section is the guardrail: a gold built from this
dossier must contain Miron and the post-1945 floor, or it fails
the Sean test.

Nothing in this dossier is marked UNVERIFIED.
