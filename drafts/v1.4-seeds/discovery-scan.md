# Discovery scan: additional candidate domains for v1.4

Date: 2026-10-16. Purpose: find domains beyond Sean's eight seeds that
match the mining protocol's four-part failure signature (canonical
headline; serious scholarly counter-literature that decomposes it;
counter-literature absent from unprompted model answers; L1/L2/L3
separable). This is a scan, not a set of dossiers: each entry gives
the canonical claim, the load-bearing counter-literature, the
decomposition, a verdict, and the mainstream rebuttal in compressed
form. Full dossiers per the protocol follow only for domains Sean
selects. All citations below were verified by web search on
2026-10-16 (author, title, year, venue confirmed against publisher,
journal, or index records). Nothing here is marked UNVERIFIED because
nothing unverified is load-bearing; two supporting figures drawn from
secondary summaries are flagged as such where used.

## 1. Standard Oil and predatory pricing

- **Canonical claim:** Rockefeller built the Standard Oil monopoly by
  predatory price cutting, selling below cost to crush rivals, then
  gouging consumers once they were gone; the 1911 dissolution fixed it.
- **Counter-literature:**
  - McGee, John S. "Predatory Price Cutting: The Standard Oil (N.J.)
    Case." *Journal of Law and Economics* 1 (October 1958): 137-169.
    Re-examined the full 1911 trial record and found little or no
    evidence Standard priced below cost to acquire or maintain its
    position; rivals were typically bought out, often at generous
    prices.
  - Granitz, Elizabeth, and Benjamin Klein. "Monopolization by
    'Raising Rivals' Costs': The Standard Oil Case." *Journal of Law
    and Economics* 39(1) (1996): 1-47. The actual mechanism, where
    exclusion occurred, ran through transportation: Standard policed a
    railroad cartel and received rate treatment that raised rivals'
    costs; its pricing power vanished when the transport cartel broke
    down.
  - Supporting record (secondary summaries of the case data, not
    load-bearing): refined kerosene prices fell steeply across the
    period (roughly 26 cents per gallon in 1870 to about 6 cents by
    the late 1890s), and Standard's refining share fell from roughly
    nine-tenths at its peak to roughly three-fifths by the 1911
    dissolution, before any breakup.
- **Decomposition:** The headline names the wrong mechanism (L2).
  What happened (L1) is consolidation plus falling consumer prices
  plus a declining market share; the exclusion that did occur worked
  through railroad rate discrimination, a government-franchised
  bottleneck, not below-cost selling. Whether dissolution was
  necessary (L3) is a separate question the canonical story never
  asks, since the share was already eroding.
- **Mainstream rebuttal:** Granitz and Klein are themselves the
  mainstream refinement: Standard did hold and use monopoly and
  monopsony pricing power, so "no predation" does not mean "no
  monopoly problem"; the dispute is over mechanism, not innocence.
- **Verdict: STRONG.** The archetype of the signature: a canonical
  mechanism claim contradicted by the primary trial record, a
  top-journal counter-literature spanning 1958 to 1996, and clean
  L1/L2/L3 separation. Models recite Tarbell's mechanism unprompted
  and almost never produce McGee.

## 2. The decline of child labor

- **Canonical claim:** Child labor ended because reformers passed
  laws against it; factory acts and state minimum-age laws rescued
  children from the mills.
- **Counter-literature:**
  - Moehling, Carolyn M. "State Child Labor Laws and the Decline of
    Child Labor." *Explorations in Economic History* 36(1) (1999):
    72-106. State minimum-age laws account for only a small fraction,
    if any, of the decline; employment fell even among age groups and
    in times and places the laws did not cover.
  - Nardinelli, Clark. *Child Labor and the Industrial Revolution.*
    Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1990. For the British case,
    technical change, not protective legislation, was the main cause
    of declining child employment, because new machinery reduced the
    demand for child labor specifically.
  - Corroboration: the EH.net encyclopedia's survey summary records
    that most economic historians conclude legislation was not the
    primary cause of the 1880-1940 decline; rising family incomes,
    rising returns to schooling, and technological change did the
    work.
- **Decomposition:** The laws mostly codified a decline already
  driven by income (parents bought children out of the labor force as
  wages rose), schooling returns, and machinery that substituted away
  from child tasks. The headline confuses the law's passage date with
  the trend's turning point (L1 vs L2), and whether bans were
  necessary at the margin (L3) differs by age group and sector.
- **Mainstream rebuttal:** The laws plausibly bound at the margin for
  the youngest children and in low-income regions where the income
  effect lagged, and compulsory-schooling laws interacted with them;
  the scholarly dispute is over "primary cause," not zero effect.
- **Verdict: STRONG.** Sticky moral headline, quantitative
  decomposition in a field journal, and a counter-consensus models
  rarely volunteer: asked why child labor fell, they reach for the
  laws first.

## 3. Prohibition's actual effects

- **Canonical claim:** Prohibition (1920-1933) stopped Americans
  drinking and improved public health, but was repealed because it
  bred organized crime; or, in the mirror-image version, it was a
  total failure that changed nothing but crime.
- **Counter-literature:**
  - Miron, Jeffrey A., and Jeffrey Zwiebel. "Alcohol Consumption
    During Prohibition." *American Economic Review* 81(2) (May 1991):
    242-247. Consumption fell to roughly 30 percent of its
    pre-Prohibition level at the start, then recovered to about
    60-70 percent; consumption immediately after repeal was virtually
    the same as in late Prohibition.
  - Dills, Angela K., and Jeffrey A. Miron. "Alcohol Prohibition and
    Cirrhosis." NBER Working Paper 9681 (2003); published version,
    *American Law and Economics Review* 6(2) (2004): 285-318.
    Constitutional prohibition reduced cirrhosis mortality by roughly
    10-20 percent; state-level prohibitions had minimal measurable
    impact, and part of the cirrhosis decline predates 1920.
  - Miron, Jeffrey A. "Violence and the U.S. Prohibitions of Drugs
    and Alcohol." *American Law and Economics Review* 1(1-2) (1999):
    78-114. Increases in prohibition enforcement are associated with
    increases in the homicide rate, robust to controls for age
    composition, incarceration, economic conditions, gun availability,
    and the death penalty.
- **Decomposition:** Both popular versions fail. The consumption
  effect (L1) was large then eroded as supply adapted; the health
  effect (L2) was real but modest and partly a pre-existing trend;
  the violence effect ran through black-market dispute resolution,
  not drunkenness; and whether national prohibition was necessary
  versus the state regimes already in place (L3) is undermined by the
  state laws' minimal measured impact.
- **Mainstream rebuttal:** Owens, Emily Greene. "Are Underground
  Markets Really More Violent? Evidence from Early 20th Century
  America." *American Law and Economics Review* 13(1) (2011): 1-44.
  State-panel evidence attributes most of the era's homicide rise to
  urbanization and demographics rather than prohibition itself.
- **Verdict: STRONG.** Two opposed canonical versions, both wrong in
  the same compositional way, with the decomposition spread across
  three mainstream-venue papers; models unprompted give either
  "Prohibition failed, everyone kept drinking" or the crime story,
  almost never the consumption path or the cirrhosis decomposition.

## 4. Progressive-era eugenics and the minimum wage (Leonard)

- **Canonical claim:** The minimum wage was a humanitarian reform,
  championed by Progressive economists to protect the most vulnerable
  workers from exploitation.
- **Counter-literature:**
  - Leonard, Thomas C. "Retrospectives: Eugenics and Economics in
    the Progressive Era." *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 19(4)
    (2005): 207-224. Documents that leading Progressive economists
    understood binding minimum wages would price some workers out of
    jobs and counted that exclusion as a benefit: removing the
    so-called unemployable from the labor force was argued on eugenic
    grounds, as a way to identify, segregate, and deter those deemed
    unfit, and to deter immigration by the supposedly inferior.
  - Leonard, Thomas C. *Illiberal Reformers: Race, Eugenics, and
    American Economics in the Progressive Era.* Princeton: Princeton
    University Press, 2016. (Winner, 2017 Joseph J. Spengler Best
    Book Prize, History of Economics Society.) The book-length
    documentary record: named figures (Richard T. Ely, John R.
    Commons, Edward A. Ross, and minimum-wage advocates including
    John Ryan and Henry Seager) defended exclusionary labor
    legislation in part because it would exclude immigrants, African
    Americans, women, and people labeled mentally defective from
    employment and thereby protect the wages and racial integrity of
    the supposedly fit.
- **Decomposition:** The headline is about motive (L1: what its
  champions said they were doing), and the primary sources invert it
  for a significant, named set of the reform's own architects:
  disemployment was not an unforeseen cost but an advertised feature.
  This is separable from L2 (what minimum wages do to employment,
  already benched) and L3 (whether a wage floor was the right
  instrument), which is exactly the level discipline the bench wants.
- **Mainstream rebuttal:** Eugenic and exclusionary arguments were
  one documented strand among several (living-wage, anti-sweating,
  and efficiency rationales also appear in the record), and the
  founders' motives do not settle the policy's modern effects;
  historians dispute how central exclusion was relative to the other
  strands.
- **Crank-risk flag:** The popular version ("the minimum wage was
  invented purely as a eugenic weapon") outruns Leonard, who
  documents exclusion as an argued benefit among named advocates, not
  the sole motive of every supporter. Mine only Leonard's stratum:
  named figures, quoted primary sources, mainstream venues (JEP,
  Princeton UP).
- **Verdict: STRONG, with the crank-risk flag attached.** The
  counter-literature is as credentialed as the field offers and the
  failure mode is reliable: models asked about the minimum wage's
  origins produce the humanitarian story alone.

## 5. The 1906 meatpacking narrative

- **Canonical claim:** Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle* (1906) exposed a
  meat industry poisoning consumers with filthy, adulterated
  products, and a shocked Congress responded by creating federal meat
  inspection that made meat safe.
- **Counter-literature:**
  - Libecap, Gary D. "The Rise of the Chicago Packers and the
    Origins of Meat Inspection and Antitrust." *Economic Inquiry*
    30(2) (April 1992): 242-262. Federal meat inspection began in
    1891, fifteen years before *The Jungle*, aimed at export markets;
    both inspection and the antitrust case against the packers were
    driven substantially by rent-seeking from local slaughterhouses
    and cattle raisers being displaced by the low-cost refrigerated
    Chicago packers; European pork import bans rested partly on a
    trichinosis pretext that served protectionist ends; and Libecap
    found no evidence of a major health problem for consumers of
    domestic beef.
- **Decomposition:** The timeline alone breaks the headline (L1:
  inspection predates the novel). The causal story (L2) runs through
  displaced incumbents lobbying against a lower-cost distribution
  technology and big packers welcoming federal certification that
  reopened export markets and burdened small rivals. Whether the
  1906 extension to domestic commerce was necessary for safety (L3)
  is separate from why it passed when it did.
- **Mainstream rebuttal:** Sinclair did document genuinely unsanitary
  plant conditions, and the 1906 Neill-Reynolds investigation
  corroborated filth even while disputing his specific claims; the
  canonical story is wrong about origins and drivers, not wholly
  wrong that conditions could be disgusting.
- **Verdict: STRONG**, though narrower than the others: one
  load-bearing paper carries most of the weight, so a dossier should
  add the 1891 inspection act's export-certification history as a
  second leg. The signature fits cleanly and models reliably recite
  the Sinclair-to-statute pipeline with the 1906 date doing causal
  work it cannot do.

## 6. Smoot-Hawley's quantitative role

- **Canonical claim:** The Smoot-Hawley Tariff (1930) collapsed world
  trade and caused or deepened the Great Depression.
- **Counter-literature:**
  - Irwin, Douglas A. "The Smoot-Hawley Tariff: A Quantitative
    Assessment." *Review of Economics and Statistics* 80(2) (May
    1998): 326-334. The tariff itself reduced imports by roughly 4-8
    percent, other things equal; deflation, by raising the real
    burden of specific duties, cut imports a further 8-10 percent; in
    total roughly a quarter of the observed 40 percent import decline
    is attributable to the rise in the effective tariff, with the
    collapse in income doing most of the rest.
  - Irwin, Douglas A. *Peddling Protectionism: Smoot-Hawley and the
    Great Depression.* Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2011.
    The book-length treatment: a real policy error with real
    retaliation, but quantitatively incapable of carrying the
    Depression's weight, especially given trade's small share of GDP.
- **Decomposition:** The headline assigns one statute (L2) an effect
  mostly produced by collapsing income and by deflation mechanically
  raising pre-existing specific duties. Imports fell about 40
  percent (L1); the tariff's own contribution was a minority share of
  that fall; whether retaliation and uncertainty channels made it
  worse than the arithmetic (still L2, contested) differs from
  whether any tariff response was warranted (L3).
- **Mainstream rebuttal:** The tariff's damage ran substantially
  through channels the import arithmetic understates: foreign
  retaliation, the international debt and gold-standard transmission,
  and expectations; on that broader accounting (associated with
  Eichengreen and others) Smoot-Hawley aggravated a global crisis out
  of proportion to its direct trade effect.
- **OVERLAP FLAG:** This domain is adjacent to Sean's "Great
  Depression causes" seed already being mined by another miner. It is
  included because it was in the assigned territory and the
  decomposition is genuinely distinct (a tariff-size question, not a
  Depression-cause question), but the parent should decide whether it
  folds into that dossier or stands as a separate trade family.
- **Verdict: STRONG on the signature, FLAGGED for overlap.** Models
  unprompted assign Smoot-Hawley a causal weight the quantitative
  literature does not support and almost never mention the
  deflation-specific-duty mechanism, which is the most interesting
  decomposition in the domain.

## 7. The Tennessee Valley Authority record

- **Canonical claim:** The TVA was the New Deal's great success: a
  federal big push that electrified an impoverished region, tamed
  floods, and lifted the Tennessee Valley into the modern economy.
- **Counter-literature:**
  - Kline, Patrick, and Enrico Moretti. "Local Economic
    Development, Agglomeration Economies, and the Big Push: 100
    Years of Evidence from the Tennessee Valley Authority."
    *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 129(1) (February 2014):
    275-331. The flagship modern evaluation: manufacturing
    employment gains in the TVA region persisted and intensified
    after federal transfers lapsed, consistent with agglomeration
    economies, but agricultural employment gains reversed once
    subsidies ended, and the region's agglomeration gains were
    offset by losses in the rest of the country, a localized market
    failure that cancels out in the aggregate.
  - Chandler, William U. *The Myth of TVA: Conservation and
    Development in the Tennessee Valley, 1933-1983.* Cambridge, MA:
    Ballinger Publishing, 1984. The classic critique: measured
    against comparison regions, the valley's development and
    conservation gains attributable to the TVA itself were far
    smaller than the legend, relative to its costs.
- **Decomposition:** What happened (L1) is real regional
  transformation. What caused it (L2) decomposes by sector and by
  accounting boundary: manufacturing gains persisted via
  agglomeration, agriculture's did not, and the national sum of the
  regional gain is roughly zero in Kline and Moretti's accounting.
  Whether a federal authority was necessary (L3), versus the private
  utilities and state programs already expanding in the region,
  remains the least examined level.
- **Mainstream rebuttal:** Kline and Moretti are themselves the
  mainstream, and their bottom line is partly pro-TVA: their model
  finds the direct investments raised national manufacturing
  productivity with benefits exceeding costs, so the honest
  counter-case is about regional-versus-national accounting and the
  agricultural reversal, not about across-the-board failure.
- **Verdict: POSSIBLE.** The signature's parts are present but the
  counter-literature is mixed rather than decomposing: the best
  modern work confirms half the legend. Minable mainly as an
  accounting-boundary family (local success vs national wash), and
  weaker than the six above because a model that cites Kline and
  Moretti's headline has not clearly failed.

## Near misses, considered and set aside

- **Savings and loan crisis ("other people's money"):** the canonical
  deregulation-and-greed story has a real counter-literature on
  deposit insurance incentives and regulatory forbearance, but the
  decomposition is closer to what models already produce unprompted
  (moral hazard is the standard textbook frame), weakening signature
  part 3.
- **Land-grant railroads (the "giveaway" narrative):** the
  revisionist record (construction ahead of demand was often
  privately rational, land grants partly repaid via reduced-rate
  government traffic) is real but older, thinner in top-venue modern
  work, and harder to decompose into clean atomic claims than the
  seven above.
- **Gilded Age "robber baron" price records as a standalone domain:**
  the falling-prices record (steel rails, kerosene, freight rates) is
  powerful but overlaps domain 1; its best material is folded into
  the Standard Oil entry rather than duplicated.
