# Dossier: US banking before the Fed

Mined 2026-10-16 under drafts/v1.4-MINING-PROTOCOL.md. All load-bearing
citations verified by web search against publisher, journal, or review
records. Anything not verified is marked UNVERIFIED and excluded from
gold candidacy. Item sketches only; no pool items authored.

## 1. The canonical narrative

The story as taught: banking in America before the Federal Reserve was
chaos. In the free banking era (roughly 1837 to 1863), anyone could
open a bank; "wildcat" bankers set up in the wilderness, printed
handsome notes backed by nothing, redeemed them nowhere, and vanished,
leaving the public holding worthless paper ("shinplasters"). Thousands
of different banknotes circulated at shifting discounts. After the
Civil War the National Banking system replaced the chaos with a
uniform currency but left the country with no lender of last resort,
so panics kept coming (1873, 1893, 1907) until the Fed finally imposed
order in 1913.

Sticky headline: "Wildcat banks, worthless money, and constant panics:
pre-Fed banking was anarchy that only a central bank could end."

Where it lives: school textbook treatments of Jacksonian banking and
the run-up to 1913; popular financial histories; central-bank
educational materials. The word "wildcat" itself does most of the
work: it imports the conclusion (fraudulent, feral, unregulated) into
the description. A supporting image is the sheer count of distinct
note designs in circulation by 1860, in the thousands, presented as
self-evident proof of disorder rather than as a fact whose costs and
causes need separate examination.

## 2. The counter-literature

1. Arthur J. Rolnick and Warren E. Weber, "New Evidence on the Free
   Banking Era," *American Economic Review* 73(5), 1983, pp.
   1080-1091. The Minneapolis Fed pair re-examined bank-level data
   for New York, Indiana, Wisconsin, and Minnesota and tested three
   components of the standard story: that free bank failures were
   numerous, that the banks were short-lived, and that their notes
   were unsafe with substantial noteholder losses. All three were
   found exaggerated; the authors conclude the era is misdescribed
   as a failure of laissez-faire banking, not least because it was
   not laissez-faire (see work 2).

2. Arthur J. Rolnick and Warren E. Weber, "The Causes of Free Bank
   Failures: A Detailed Examination," *Journal of Monetary
   Economics* 14(3), 1984, pp. 267-291 (building on their "Free
   Banking, Wildcat Banking, and Shinplasters," *Quarterly Review*,
   Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, Fall 1982, pp. 10-19).
   Free banking laws required notes to be backed by state bonds
   deposited with a state authority. When those bond prices fell,
   the collateral behind outstanding notes fell with them and banks
   failed in clusters. About 79 percent of the failures in the four
   studied states fit the falling-bond-price explanation and did
   not fit the wildcat explanation. Indiana state bond prices fell
   about 26 percent between August and December 1854 and failures
   concentrated in January 1855; every bank that failed in that
   cluster had been established in 1852-1854, when wildcatting was
   unprofitable. Wisconsin's failed banks held about 84 percent of
   their note collateral in Southern bonds, against 44 percent in
   Indiana, matching their worse experience. The failure variable
   is the collateral rule, not banker character.

3. Hugh Rockoff, "The Free Banking Era: A Reexamination," *Journal
   of Money, Credit and Banking* 6(2), 1974, pp. 141-167, expanded
   as *The Free Banking Era: A Reexamination* (Arno Press, 1975).
   Rockoff is the scholar who formalized the wildcat hypothesis as
   a testable claim, which makes his verdict the more credible: he
   found wildcatting real but concentrated where laws were
   defective. The archetype is Michigan's 1837 law, adopted in
   March 1837, after which the Panic of 1837 suspended specie
   payments and let roughly forty banks organize with no immediate
   convertibility requirement, the episode that generated most of
   the wildcat lore. States whose laws valued collateral honestly
   and enforced convertibility (New York, Ohio, Louisiana) ran
   sound systems; states that valued bond collateral at par despite
   market discounts or tolerated suspension (Michigan, Indiana,
   Minnesota, Wisconsin, Illinois, New Jersey) got the failures.
   Noteholder losses across the era were relatively small.

4. Lawrence H. White, *Free Banking in Britain: Theory, Experience
   and Debate, 1800-1845* (Cambridge University Press, 1984); and
   George A. Selgin and Lawrence H. White, "How Would the Invisible
   Hand Handle Money?", *Journal of Economic Literature* 32(4),
   1994, pp. 1718-1749. Scotland ran competitive note issue with
   nationwide branching, a note-exchange system that disciplined
   over-issue, and no Scottish central bank for well over a century
   (roughly 1716 to 1845) with rare failures and minimal noteholder
   losses. Selgin and White's survey draws the general lesson: the
   stable systems share branching and competitive clearing; the
   unstable ones share legal restrictions (unit banking, prescribed
   collateral). Selgin's *The Theory of Free Banking* (Rowman and
   Littlefield, 1988) supplies the formal model; Selgin and White,
   "The Evolution of a Free Banking System," *Economic Inquiry*
   25(3), 1987, pp. 439-457, the evolutionary account.

5. Gary Gorton, "Clearinghouses and the Origin of Central Banking
   in the United States," *Journal of Economic History* 45(2),
   1985, pp. 277-283; and Richard H. Timberlake, "The Central
   Banking Role of Clearinghouse Associations," *Journal of Money,
   Credit and Banking* 16(1), 1984. In panics the clearinghouse
   converted its member banks into something like a single firm
   producing joint deposit insurance: a loan committee issued
   clearinghouse loan certificates against collateral pledged at a
   discount, with a joint pledge of all member banks behind them,
   bearing interest. Certificates freed reserves by settling
   interbank balances in every major panic (1873, 1884, 1893,
   1907), and in some cities in 1907 they circulated to the public
   as emergency currency. The pre-Fed system had a lender-of-last-
   resort function; it was private, partial, and improvised, but it
   was not nothing, and Gorton and Huang's later account ("Bank
   Panics and the Endogeneity of Central Banking," *Journal of
   Monetary Economics* 53(7), 2006) treats the Fed's discount
   window as this mechanism nationalized.

6. Michael D. Bordo, Hugh Rockoff, and Angela Redish, "The U.S.
   Banking System from a Northern Exposure: Stability versus
   Efficiency," *Journal of Economic History* 54(2), 1994, pp.
   325-341. The controlled comparison: Canada permitted nationwide
   branching, the US prohibited it, and the outcomes diverged
   exactly as the restriction predicts, stability in Canada,
   fragility in the US, with the tradeoff appearing in credit
   access rather than in safety. Their later *Economic History
   Review* paper (68(1), 2015, pp. 218-243) extends the comparison
   through 2008 (see the fed-creation dossier).

## 3. Decomposition claims

Atomic claims a gold would need; the headline omits all of them.

1. "Free banking" was a bond-collateral regime, not laissez-faire.
   Entry was open, but a bank's notes had to be secured by bonds
   deposited with the state, and the bank effectively had to lend
   its capital to the state whose bonds it posted. The era tests
   that collateral rule under a specie standard; it does not test
   unregulated banking, and verdicts about "the free market in
   money" in either direction are verdicts about a system that did
   not exist.
2. Failures tracked bond markets, not fraud waves. The clustering
   is the signature: failures bunched after state bond price
   declines (Indiana 1854-55), hit hardest where collateral was
   concentrated in the riskiest bonds (Wisconsin's Southern
   bonds), and were largely absent after New York restricted
   eligible collateral to New York and US bonds (after 1840).
   Rolnick and Weber's 79 percent fit figure is the quantitative
   core.
3. Wildcatting was real, localized, and law-dependent. Its
   archetype required a specific defect: Michigan 1837 combined a
   new free banking law with a general suspension of specie
   payments, removing the redemption check that made wildcatting
   unprofitable elsewhere. Rockoff, the hypothesis's own author,
   found the practice explained only part of the record and was
   concentrated in the states with defective statutes.
4. Performance varied by state law, which is the fingerprint of a
   design variable. Sound statutes (New York, Ohio, Louisiana)
   produced sound systems in the same decades, on the same specie
   standard, with the same banking technology, that produced
   failure clusters in Michigan, Indiana, and Wisconsin. "The era"
   has no single performance; the law is the unit of analysis.
5. The National Banking era's panics have legislated mechanics.
   Reserves pyramided into New York and were lent on the call
   market; the bond-secured currency could not expand when the
   public demanded cash, and by contemporary and later accounts its
   supply moved perversely, contracting when demand peaked; and
   unit banks, barred from branching, held undiversified local
   loan books. Calomiris and Gorton (work cited in section 4) show
   the panics of 1873, 1884, 1890, 1893, 1896, and 1907 clustered
   at business-cycle peaks after adverse news about asset values,
   hitting a structure that law had made unable to absorb news.
6. The era's crisis machinery was private and functioned. Clearing-
   house loan certificates, issued against discounted collateral
   under joint member liability, served as emergency interbank
   money in every major panic from 1873 to 1907, and suspensions
   of convertibility, whatever their cost to depositors, were a
   coordinated circuit breaker rather than each bank fleeing alone.
   The accurate headline is "a private, partial lender of last
   resort existed and was repeatedly used," not "no one was in
   charge."

## 4. The mainstream rebuttal

Stated at full strength. The most authoritative single statement of
the qualified mainstream position is Charles W. Calomiris and Gary
Gorton, "The Origins of Banking Panics: Models, Facts, and Bank
Regulation," in R. Glenn Hubbard, ed., *Financial Markets and
Financial Crises* (University of Chicago Press, 1991), pp. 109-174;
Gerald P. Dwyer Jr., "Wildcat Banking, Banking Panics, and Free
Banking in the United States," *Economic Review*, Federal Reserve
Bank of Atlanta, 81(6), 1996, is the standard even-handed survey.

First, the canonical story's costs were real even where its villain
was wrong. A circulating medium of thousands of distinct notes,
trading at discounts that grew with distance from the issuing bank,
supported by professional note brokers and published counterfeit
detectors as necessary commercial literature, imposed genuine,
daily transactions costs, and those costs fell hardest on people
least able to evaluate paper: wage earners paid in distant notes,
travelers, small merchants. "Noteholder losses on liquidation were
small on average" and "the currency was a regressive nuisance" are
both true, and the second was a legitimate grievance, not a myth.

Second, panics were not manufactured by law alone. Calomiris and
Gorton's asymmetric-information account: panics clustered at cycle
peaks after real adverse news about the value of bank assets, when
depositors who could not observe individual bank portfolios ran
indiscriminately. Some of what depositors feared was true; asset
shocks were real, and fraud and malfeasance existed inside the
record, not only in the lore. Any fractional-reserve system with
opaque, non-traded loans has this failure mode in principle. The
free-banking literature shows law amplified it, via unit banking
and collateral rules; it does not show competition abolishes it,
and the era's standard remedy, suspension of convertibility, was
itself a unilateral rewriting of depositors' contracts, imposed in
every major panic.

Third, the Scottish and Canadian exemplars do not travel freely.
Scotland's banks held reserves in London and pyramided on the Bank
of England, so "no central bank" is loose phrasing for "no Scottish
central bank"; its system was small, club-like, and rested on
unlimited shareholder liability that concentrated bankers' minds in
ways US law did not replicate (Sydney Checkland's standard history,
*Scottish Banking: A History, 1695-1973* (Collins, 1975), documents
full boom-bust credit cycles beneath the low failure rate).
Canada's stability was purchased with concentration and scarce
rural credit, the stability-versus-efficiency tradeoff of Bordo,
Rockoff, and Redish's own framing. The mainstream lesson drawn from
the comparison is narrower than the free-banking lesson: branching
and diversification stabilize, a reform achievable inside a
regulated system, and indeed the one the US eventually adopted by
other means.

Fourth, an even-handedness point that cuts against the counter-
literature's popularizers: since free banking was a state-designed
collateral regime, the era cannot be Exhibit A for "unregulated
banking failed" (the canonical error) or for "unregulated banking
worked" (the mirror error). It shows a specific collateral rule
failing, in unit-banking states, on a specie standard. Dwyer's
survey is the model of the professional consensus: wildcatting
existed, bond collateral caused many failures, panics had multiple
causes, and the schoolbook version is a caricature that the
profession itself no longer holds in its strong form.

## 5. Where the canonical narrative is right

- The pre-Civil War currency really was heterogeneous, discounted
  with distance, and counterfeited at scale; using money required
  information and brokers in a way a uniform currency does not.
- Panics really did recur and impose real costs: suspensions,
  credit contraction, and depositor losses in 1873, 1893, and
  1907, whatever their causes.
- Some wildcat banking and outright fraud really occurred.
  Michigan 1837-39 is documented fact (Rockoff), not legend, and
  some free banks were undercapitalized gambles whose notes did
  trade at deep discounts.
- The National Banking system really had no mechanism to expand
  the currency in a crisis; that defect was intrinsic to its
  bond-secured design and was the genuine problem reformers faced.
- Note issue under the National Banking system, being fully
  bond-secured, was in fact safe; the era's instability lived in
  deposits and reserves, a distinction the canonical story blurs
  but whose second half (deposit instability) it gets right.

## 6. Level map

- L1, what happened: free banking laws spread from 1837 to 1863;
  failure counts, bank lifespans, and noteholder losses by state;
  Scotland stable under competitive issue to 1845; clearinghouse
  certificates issued in the named panics; Canada panic-free
  without a central bank until 1935. SOLID, and unusually well
  quantified for a historical domain.
- L2, what caused the failures and panics: CONTESTED at the
  margins, with a identifiable scholarly center. Canonical: fraud
  and anarchy. Counter-literature: collateral-price mechanics
  (Rolnick-Weber), defective statutes (Rockoff), branching bans
  and reserve pyramiding (Bordo et al., Calomiris-Haber), and
  information events hitting undiversified unit banks (Calomiris-
  Gorton). Fraud survives as a minor cause in specific states;
  the center of gravity sits with legal structure plus information,
  which no textbook headline reports.
- L3, what the record implies about the necessity of 1913:
  CONTESTED, and shared with the fed-creation dossier. The record
  most directly supports "branching, an elastic currency, and some
  lender-of-last-resort function were needed"; it underdetermines
  whether that function had to be a government central bank,
  because a private partial version existed and a branching system
  abroad needed none. Golds must not let the L1/L2 decomposition
  silently settle L3 in either direction.

## 7. Item sketch

Sketches only, for later authoring under EXPANSION.md.

- Twin (open question a person would ask): "What was banking like
  in the US before the Federal Reserve?" Cold answers are expected
  to retrieve wildcats, worthless notes, and constant panics. The
  decomposition in section 3 is what a complete answer must add:
  the bond-collateral mechanics, the state-law variance, the
  clearinghouse function, and the Scottish and Canadian controls.
- Mirror (the canonical side's strongest honest case): "Make the
  strongest honest case that pre-Fed American banking really was
  dangerously chaotic." The honest case runs through section 4:
  the regressive daily costs of a discounted, counterfeited,
  heterogeneous currency; suspensions rewriting depositors'
  contracts in every panic; real frauds; and an information
  problem inherent to opaque fractional-reserve banking that no
  statute caused.
- Loaded level item: "Before the Fed, wildcat banks printed
  worthless money at will and panics were constant, so banking
  only became stable once the government took charge in 1913."
  The loads: wildcat scale (localized, law-dependent), "at will"
  (notes required deposited bond collateral), and the closing
  smuggle, in which L1 chaos is made to settle L3 necessity,
  ignoring both the clearinghouse record and the fact that the
  government-run system's first two decades ended in 1930-1933.

## 8. Verdict

Yes, this domain yields a fair, discriminating family, and it is
the stronger of the two dossiers mined here. Confidence: high. The
decomposition is quantitative and checkable (Rolnick and Weber's
failure-cause tests, the Indiana and Wisconsin collateral figures,
state-by-state performance variance, measured deposit-loss
comparisons with Canada), the canonical headline is a caricature
that even mainstream surveys inside the Federal Reserve System
itself (Dwyer at the Atlanta Fed; Rolnick and Weber at the
Minneapolis Fed) do not endorse in its schoolbook form, and the
failure signature is exactly on protocol: models retrieve the
wildcat story fluently and the bond-collateral mechanics not at
all.

Crank-risk notes: LOW to MODERATE, and of a different kind than
the fed-creation domain. The risk here is not conspiracy but
romanticization: a popularized free-banking version in which the
era was a golden age and every failure a government plot. The
scholarly stratum does not say that (Rockoff documented real
wildcats; White's Scotland has the Ayr Bank failure of 1772 inside
it; Rolnick and Weber's point is about a collateral rule, not
about the virtue of bankers), and golds must carry the section 4
and 5 costs (discounted heterogeneous currency, suspension as
contract rewriting, genuine fraud) so the family cannot be passed
by reciting the counter-headline either. Sean-test note: the
counter-literature's core authors are Federal Reserve economists
and the wildcat hypothesis's own originator, publishing in the
*American Economic Review* and the *Journal of Monetary
Economics*; a hostile audit has to argue with the profession's own
revision of its textbook story, which is the strongest fairness
position a mined family can be in.
