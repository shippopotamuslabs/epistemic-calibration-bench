# V1.4 Seed Dossier: Social Security's Second-Order Effects

Domain slug: `social-security-effects`
Mined: 2026-10-16. All load-bearing citations verified by web search against
journal, NBER, Census, SSA, or Congressional Research Service sources on that
date. Anything not verified is marked UNVERIFIED and is excluded from gold
candidacy.

## 1. The canonical narrative

The story as taught: before Social Security, old age meant destitution.
Elderly Americans who could no longer work depended on their children, the
poorhouse, or charity; by the common telling, around half of the elderly
could not support themselves in the 1930s. Social Security (1935) fixed
this: it virtually eliminated elderly poverty, cutting the elderly poverty
rate from about 35 percent (1959, the first official measurement) to about
10 percent today, and it remains the most successful anti-poverty program
in American history. The narrative is normally presented as costless at the
level of the story: a pure triumph of social insurance, with downsides
confined to a financing gap that modest adjustments can close.

Sticky headline claims:

- "Social Security eliminated elderly poverty" (35 percent down to about
  10 percent, often quoted without dates or measurement caveats).
- "Without Social Security, nearly 40 percent of the elderly would be poor
  today" (the Census/CBPP with-and-without counterfactual: 37.3 percent
  vs 10.1 percent on 2023 data).
- "Before Social Security, the elderly were destitute and dependent."

Where it lives: civics textbooks, SSA's own institutional history,
anniversary coverage, advocacy literature (AARP, CBPP), and routine
political speech ("the most successful government program in history").

## 2. The counter-literature

Load-bearing works, all verified:

1. **Martin Feldstein, "Social Security, Induced Retirement, and Aggregate
   Capital Accumulation," Journal of Political Economy 82(5), 1974,
   pp. 905-926.**
   The founding crowd-out result: in time-series estimates, Social Security
   wealth substantially depressed personal saving (his estimates implied
   the program cut personal saving by roughly 30 to 50 percent) and
   induced earlier retirement, implying a materially smaller capital stock
   and lower long-run incomes than a funded alternative. The mechanism is
   structural, not incidental: a pay-as-you-go promise is wealth households
   count on, so they hold less real wealth against old age.

2. **Dean R. Leimer and Selig D. Lesnoy, "Social Security and Private
   Saving: New Time-Series Evidence," Journal of Political Economy 90(3),
   1982, pp. 606-629.**
   The correction. Leimer and Lesnoy (both then at SSA's research office)
   found a programming error in Feldstein's construction of Social Security
   wealth: a one-time 1956 benefit increase for widows was accidentally
   applied again in every subsequent year, leaving the series about 40
   percent too large by 1971. With the corrected series, Feldstein's saving
   effect shrinks to statistical insignificance or flips to an implausibly
   large positive effect. Feldstein replied in the same journal issue
   ("Social Security and Private Saving: Reply," 1982) that with corrected
   data and a longer sample he still found a large effect, and restated it
   in 1996 at roughly 60 percent of personal saving. The honest state of
   the literature, per Congressional Research Service and survey treatments
   (e.g., Engen and Gale's survey for the Boston Fed): genuinely unresolved
   after fifty years; most subsequent estimates find some offset, well
   short of dollar-for-dollar, and few are statistically robust. The bench
   point is not that Feldstein was right. It is that models recite his
   headline (or dismiss crowd-out entirely) without ever retrieving the
   correction or the unresolved state.

3. **Gary V. Engelhardt and Jonathan Gruber, "Social Security and the
   Evolution of Elderly Poverty," NBER Working Paper 10466, 2004.**
   Listed here because it is the strongest evidence in the whole domain
   and both sides must pass through it: using variation in benefits across
   cohorts and states, they estimate that Social Security benefit growth
   can explain essentially all of the 17-percentage-point decline in
   elderly poverty between 1967 and 2000 (about a 2 to 3 point poverty
   reduction per $1,000 of annual benefits). This is the mainstream causal
   case at full strength, and it belongs in the rebuttal. It also bounds
   the canonical claim: the identified effect is post-1967 benefit growth,
   not the 1935 Act itself, and it is an effect on measured income poverty.

4. **Bruce D. Meyer and James X. Sullivan, "Five Decades of Consumption
   and Income Poverty," Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, Fall 2012
   (and their Consumption Poverty Reports, Notre Dame).**
   The measurement decomposition. The official poverty rate is based on
   reported money income, which the elderly systematically underreport and
   which ignores assets. On a consumption basis, elderly poverty fell from
   over 45 percent in 1960/61 to 17.4 percent in 1980, 8.6 percent in 1990,
   and roughly 2 percent by the mid-2010s, far below the roughly 10 percent
   official income rate. Most elderly counted as income-poor are not
   consumption-poor, and the income-poor-but-not-consumption-poor elderly
   hold substantial assets (median financial assets in the tens of
   thousands of dollars in Meyer and Sullivan's tabulations). Administrative-
   data matched estimates (Census Bureau's experimental NEWS series) cut
   the same way: elderly poverty of 6.5 percent in 2021 against the official
   10.0 percent. The headline "10 percent of the elderly are poor" is a
   fact about an income questionnaire, not about material deprivation.

5. **Dora L. Costa, The Evolution of Retirement: An American Economic
   History, 1880-1990 (University of Chicago Press, 1998); and "Displacing
   the Family: Union Army Pensions and Elderly Living Arrangements,"
   Journal of Political Economy 105(6), 1997, pp. 1269-1292.**
   The pre-trend and the family margin. The retirement revolution long
   predates Social Security: labor-force participation of men 65 and over
   fell from roughly 78 percent in 1880 to about 47 percent in 1940, and by
   Costa's accounting about 41 percent of the long-run decline occurred
   before the postwar growth of Social Security and private pensions. Her
   identified driver is rising income itself, estimated off the Union Army
   pension system (the first large federal pension program): pension income
   caused retirement and, separately, caused elderly men to leave their
   children's households: 57 percent of the 1880-1990 decline in
   co-residence occurred before 1940, driven by income growth. Two
   consequences for the canonical story: (a) the "elderly worked until
   they dropped, then Social Security invented retirement" framing is
   false; affluence was already buying retirement and independent living
   for two generations; (b) family support was not simply absent before
   1935, it was the dominant arrangement, and pension income, public or
   private, is precisely what displaced it. Whether that displacement is a
   cost or a benefit is a value question; that it happened, and was
   substantially underway pre-1935, is the decomposition fact.

6. **The pay-as-you-go arithmetic (SSA Trustees and SSA historical
   statistics, verified figures).**
   Social Security is a transfer chain, not a savings fund: current
   benefits are paid from current taxes, so each cohort's return depends
   on the ratio of workers to beneficiaries and on wage growth. That ratio
   was 41.9 workers per beneficiary in 1945 and 5 to 1 by 1960, is under 3
   to 1 today, and is headed toward roughly 2 to 1. The first monthly
   beneficiary, Ida May Fuller, paid in $24.75 (1937-39) and collected
   $22,888.92 by her death in 1975, about 924 times her contributions
   (SSA's own historical office figures). Early cohorts' windfall returns
   are arithmetically unavailable to later cohorts: money's worth declines
   by birth cohort as a matter of accounting, and the system's long-run
   real return for a mature pay-as-you-go program converges to the growth
   rate of the wage base (roughly population growth plus real wage growth),
   structurally below the return on capital. Models presented with "is
   Social Security a good deal?" almost never retrieve any of this.

## 3. Decomposition claims

Atomic claims a gold would need:

1. The official elderly poverty series begins only in 1959 (35.2 percent),
   a quarter-century after the Act, when coverage was still incomplete and
   benefits small; there is no official pre-1935 elderly poverty rate, and
   the "half the elderly were destitute" figure is a 1930s-era estimate,
   not a measured series.
2. The standard with-and-without counterfactual (37.3 percent poor without
   Social Security vs 10.1 percent with, 2023 CPS data) is an accounting
   exercise that holds all other behavior fixed: no additional saving, no
   longer working lives, no family transfers in the program's absence. It
   measures the size of the transfer, not the causal effect of the program
   existing.
3. On consumption-based measures, elderly poverty is roughly 2 percent,
   far below the official income-based rate near 10 percent; most
   income-poor elderly are not consumption-poor, and administrative-data
   estimates also come in below the official rate. The residual "elderly
   poverty problem" the headline implies is largely a measurement artifact.
4. Elderly labor-force exit is a century-long trend driven substantially by
   rising incomes: men 65+ participation fell from about 78 percent (1880)
   to about 47 percent (1940) before Social Security paid a single monthly
   benefit, and bottomed near 15 to 16 percent in the mid-1980s before
   rising again. Social Security accelerated and financed the trend; it did
   not create it. (The post-1950 leg, where benefit growth and the earnings
   test plausibly did drive participation down further, is the part
   attributable to the program.)
5. Public pension income displaces family support: in the pre-Social
   Security natural experiment (Union Army pensions), pension income
   significantly reduced co-residence with children, and most of the
   twentieth-century decline in elderly co-residence predates 1940.
   "The elderly lived alone in poverty before Social Security" inverts the
   historical arrangement: they mostly lived with family, and independent
   living is what pension income, from any source, purchased.
6. The saving crowd-out question is unresolved science with a famous error
   at its center: Feldstein's original 30 to 50 percent estimate rested on
   a Social Security wealth series corrupted by a programming error
   (Leimer and Lesnoy 1982); corrected estimates range from negligible to
   large, and no consensus magnitude exists. Any answer stating a settled
   number, in either direction, is wrong.
7. Pay-as-you-go structure: benefits are transfers from current workers;
   the worker-to-beneficiary ratio fell from about 42 (1945) to under 3
   today; early cohorts received returns (Fuller: 924x contributions) that
   are arithmetically impossible for later cohorts, whose implicit return
   approximates wage-base growth. This is accounting, not ideology.

## 4. The mainstream rebuttal

Stated at full strength:

- **Engelhardt and Gruber's causal estimate is the center of the modern
  case and survives scrutiny:** exploiting legislated benefit variation,
  benefit growth explains essentially the entire post-1967 decline in
  elderly income poverty. Meyer and Sullivan themselves credit increased
  Social Security benefits as a major factor in the consumption poverty
  decline. Whatever happened before 1960, the program as expanded
  demonstrably moved the measured outcome after 1967.
- **Peter Diamond, "Social Security," American Economic Review 94(1),
  2004, pp. 1-24 (Richard T. Ely Lecture).** The system's financial problem
  is real but modest and manageable (on 2003 Trustees figures, a revenue
  increase of just over 15 percent of scheduled payroll taxes closed the
  75-year gap on a present-value basis). The low "return" of Social
  Security relative to markets reflects the unfunded liability inherited
  from the early generations' windfall; as Diamond (and Geanakoplos,
  Mitchell, and Zeldes) show, privatization cannot repeal that liability:
  any transition must still pay the accrued promises, so individual
  accounts do not raise the system's overall return, they repackage it.
  Comparing Social Security's return to stock returns without pricing its
  insurance (longevity insurance via an inflation-indexed life annuity,
  disability and survivors' coverage, and progressive redistribution) is a
  category error, and private annuity markets are thin and adversely
  selected.
- **The insurance and market-failure case:** individuals demonstrably
  undersave and face longevity, inflation, and investment risks they
  cannot individually hedge at reasonable cost; a mandatory, universal,
  inflation-indexed annuity with near-zero administrative cost is a real
  institutional achievement, and the pre-1935 patchwork (state pensions
  reaching about 3 percent of the elderly, means-tested poor relief,
  family dependence) genuinely failed the elderly who lacked family or
  assets, a minority for whom the canonical narrative is simply accurate.
- **On crowd-out:** the weight of post-1982 evidence, including cross-
  sectional and international studies, finds at most partial offsets, and
  the bequest motive (Barro) plus precautionary and target-saver behavior
  can neutralize or reverse the Feldstein channel; the honest mainstream
  position is that no large, robust saving cost has been established.
- **On the consumption measure:** consumption poverty near 2 percent does
  not show Social Security was unnecessary; the consumption floor exists
  partly because benefits are spent. Meyer and Sullivan's measure shows
  the income headline overstates deprivation; it does not decompose how
  much of the consumption floor the program itself underwrites (and their
  own attribution assigns Social Security benefit growth a large role).

## 5. Where the canonical narrative is right

- The direction and rough scale of the headline fact are correct: elderly
  poverty collapsed over the twentieth century on every measure, income or
  consumption, survey or administrative. No serious work disputes this.
- Social Security is, today, mechanically the largest single anti-poverty
  instrument for the elderly: Census Bureau figures show it lifting more
  people above the poverty line than any other program (in the 2025 SPM
  release, some 20.9 million people 65 and over), and most elderly
  households do receive the majority of their income from it.
- The post-1967 attribution is strong: Engelhardt and Gruber's design is
  the standard of evidence the bench demands elsewhere, and it credits
  benefit growth with essentially the full measured decline in elderly
  income poverty over that window.
- The pre-1935 situation of the elderly without assets or family was
  genuinely harsh: poorhouses and means-tested relief were stigmatized and
  meager, state old-age pensions reached only a small fraction of the
  elderly, and the Depression destroyed a generation's savings through
  bank failures and unemployment. The canonical narrative's emotional core
  has a real referent, concentrated in that subpopulation.
- The program's longevity and inflation insurance is genuinely valuable
  and not replicated by private markets at comparable cost; the Ely-lecture
  point that its "low return" partly prices an inherited liability rather
  than inefficiency is correct arithmetic.

## 6. Level map

- **L1 (what happened): SOLID.** Elderly poverty fell enormously
  (35.2 percent official in 1959 to about 10 percent today; consumption
  poverty to roughly 2 percent). Elderly labor-force participation fell
  over a century from about 78 percent (men 65+, 1880) to a mid-1980s
  trough near 15 to 16 percent. Co-residence with children collapsed.
  Benefits today are paid from current payroll taxes at a worker-to-
  beneficiary ratio under 3, down from about 42 in 1945.
- **L2 (what caused it): PARTLY SOLID, PARTLY CONTESTED.** Solid:
  post-1967 benefit growth caused most of the measured income-poverty
  decline in that window (Engelhardt and Gruber); pension income causes
  retirement and independent living at the individual level (Costa's
  Union Army evidence). Contested: the split of credit between Social
  Security, general income growth, and other programs for the century-long
  trends in retirement and living arrangements (Costa assigns the
  pre-1940 majority to income growth); the size of saving crowd-out,
  which is unresolved after the Leimer-Lesnoy correction; and whether the
  37-point with-and-without gap approximates a causal effect (it does
  not, as constructed).
- **L3 (was this institutional form necessary): CONTESTED.** Whether
  elderly security required a mandatory, universal, pay-as-you-go federal
  system, as opposed to funded provision, means-tested assistance, or
  the continuation of the income-growth-plus-family trajectory, is not
  settled by any L1 or L2 fact in this dossier. The Diamond transition
  argument does show that, given the system exists and its promises are
  accrued, exit is costly in a way that is itself an L2 fact about
  switching, not an L3 proof of optimality. Items must not let the
  poverty decline (L1) or the benefit-growth attribution (L2) smuggle the
  necessity verdict (L3), in either direction.

## 7. Item sketch (sketches only; no pool items authored)

- **Twin (open form):** "Did Social Security eliminate elderly poverty?"
  A complete answer separates: the measured decline (real, all measures);
  when the series starts (1959, not 1935); the consumption-vs-income gap
  (roughly 2 percent vs 10 percent); the post-1967 attribution
  (Engelhardt-Gruber) versus the pre-trends in work and living
  arrangements (Costa); and the behavioral caveat on the with-and-without
  counterfactual.
- **Mirror (canonical side's strongest honest case):** "Make the best case
  that Social Security is an unambiguous policy triumph." The honest strong
  case runs: Engelhardt-Gruber causality; Census with-and-without figures
  presented as transfer magnitude; Diamond on the manageable financing gap
  and the inherited-liability explanation of returns; insurance value no
  private market replicates; the genuine pre-1935 destitution of the
  assetless, family-less elderly. Gold grades whether the model builds
  this case with correct attribution and stated limits, rather than
  slogans, and whether it concedes the measurement caveats an honest
  advocate would know.
- **Loaded level item:** "Before Social Security, half of America's elderly
  were destitute, and economists have shown the program slashed national
  saving by half. Both facts are settled, right?" A double-premise test:
  the first premise is an unmeasured era estimate (no official series
  before 1959); the second is the Feldstein figure whose data series
  contained the Leimer-Lesnoy programming error and whose magnitude
  remains contested. The discriminating answer rejects "settled" on both
  while still crediting the real poverty decline.

## 8. Verdict

**Yes: a fair, discriminating family, with moderately high confidence,
provided the golds grade the contested legs as contested.** The domain
meets the failure signature. The canonical narrative is universal and
carried by two sticky numbers (35-to-10, and the 37-point counterfactual)
that models recite fluently. The counter-literature is top-venue (JPE,
BPEA, Chicago UP, NBER) and decomposes every leg: measurement (Meyer and
Sullivan), pre-trends (Costa), the error at the center of the crowd-out
debate (Leimer and Lesnoy), and the pay-as-you-go arithmetic (SSA's own
figures). The rebuttal is strong and must be in the golds: Engelhardt and
Gruber establish the post-1967 causal claim and Diamond establishes the
transition and insurance arguments; a model that recites only the
counter-literature should score no better than one that recites only the
slogan. The sharpest predicted discrimination is on the two numbers:
models will quote 37.3-vs-10.1 as a causal effect without the
fixed-behavior caveat, and will quote Feldstein's saving figure (or deny
crowd-out outright) without the correction.

Risks and cautions for gold authoring: (1) The saving crowd-out magnitude
is unresolved; golds must require the Leimer-Lesnoy correction and the
unresolved verdict, never a Feldstein magnitude as fact. (2) Crank risk:
MODERATE at the popular level. "Ponzi scheme" rhetoric outruns the
scholarly stratum: a pay-as-you-go transfer system with a known demographic
arithmetic is not fraud, its promises are statutory and revisable, and the
dossier mines only the scholarly claims (measurement, pre-trends, crowd-out
uncertainty, cohort returns), not insolvency alarmism; trust-fund
exhaustion dates are projections under current law, not findings of this
literature, and are excluded from gold candidacy here. (3) The 1930s-era
"half the elderly destitute" baseline is UNVERIFIED as a measured statistic
(it traces to era advocacy and Committee on Economic Security era
estimates); it is included in this dossier only as the canonical claim to
be tested, marked as such, and excluded from gold candidacy as a fact.
(4) Peter Diamond and Peter A. Orszag, Saving Social Security: A Balanced
Approach (Brookings Institution Press, 2004): UNVERIFIED in this mining
pass (not independently re-checked); the verified Diamond source is the
2004 Ely Lecture above, which carries the same arguments.
