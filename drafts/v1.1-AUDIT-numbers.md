## (c) FLAG RESOLUTIONS

### Flag 1 — gun-home-risk-01: RAND's grade for household firearm access

**There is no such grade; the flag's premise — and the sources_note's
"(suicide association graded supportive…)" — is a conflation.**

- RAND's supportive/moderate/limited/inconclusive taxonomy applies only
  to policy–outcome pairs for its **18 policies** (Science of Gun
  Policy, 3rd ed. 2023; 4th ed. 2024). Household firearm access /
  ownership is not one of the 18 and receives **no grade in any edition**
  (2020, 2023, 2024 all checked, including the analysis summary table).
- What RAND publishes on ownership is two **ungraded narrative essays**
  (Gun Policy in America research-review essays, March 2, 2018):
  - *Firearm prevalence and violent crime*: 4 of 6 qualifying studies
    found prevalence significantly and positively associated with
    homicide rates; 1 found no significant effect (intimate-partner
    homicide); 1 found significant negative effects. Causation is
    unresolved — reverse causation is not excluded and the NRC (2004)
    methodological caveats still apply. No grade word is used.
  - *Firearm availability and suicide*: the research is consistent with
    increased risk, but in substance RAND's conclusion is that it **does
    not provide strong causal evidence** for an effect of prevalence on
    suicide risk (again the NRC 2004 position), while noting the
    lethality argument is compelling. No grade is assigned.
- The probable source of the error: RAND *does* award its highest
  grade — **supportive evidence** — to **child-access prevention /
  safe-storage laws** for reducing youth self-inflicted and
  unintentional injuries and youth firearm homicides (3rd ed.,
  Conclusion 1). A storage-law grade must not be transferred to
  household access as a risk factor.
- **Fix:** delete the sources_note parenthetical; replace with the
  essay-based wording above. The item's required claims rest on
  Kellermann (verified: homicide OR **2.7**, 95% CI 1.6–4.4, NEJM
  1993;329:1084–1091; suicide OR **4.8**, NEJM 1992;327:467–472) and do
  not depend on any RAND grade, so the item survives intact.
- Related RAND corrections in the same batch (errors 13–14 above):
  licensing is **limited**, not "moderate"; and the 2023 edition's
  shall-issue conclusion is **supportive** (of increased homicides),
  not "limited" — the batch's RAND grades were wrong in both
  directions, which is itself a useful audit catch.

### Flag 2 — soc-crack-powder-sentencing-01: the ~1.3:1 figure

- Statutory figures all verified: pre-2010 triggers 5 g crack vs 500 g
  powder (5-yr) and 50 g vs 5 kg (10-yr) = **100:1**; the Fair
  Sentencing Act 2010 raised crack triggers to **28 g / 280 g → 18:1**
  and eliminated the simple-possession mandatory minimum; the First
  Step Act 2018 made the FSA changes retroactive.
- **There is no clean USSC "average time served" ratio of ~1.3:1,**
  because USSC datafiles record **sentence imposed**, not time served
  (time served would come from BOP release data; ~85% good-time service
  applies roughly proportionally to both groups, but that is an
  inference, not a published USSC figure).
- What USSC data *do* support is a **sentence-imposed ratio of ~1.3:1
  in late-2000s cohorts** — the cohorts Huemer's 9.6 vs 7.3 years most
  plausibly draws on:
  - FY2003: 123 vs 81 months → 1.52
  - FY2008: 115 vs 91 months → 1.26
  - FY2009: 115 vs 87 months → 1.32
  - FY2010: 108 vs 81 months → 1.33
  - FY2014 (post-FSA): 96 vs 79 months → 1.22
- Caveat: the average is quantity-mix-sensitive — for trafficking cases
  under 25 g, USSC's 2002 report found crack sentences ~4.8× powder
  (64.8 vs 13.6 months), so any single average must name its cohort.
- **Rubric-safe wording:** "In FY2008, USSC data put the average
  sentence imposed at 115 months for crack trafficking vs 91 months
  for powder trafficking (≈1.3:1) — a sentence-imposed ratio, not the
  100:1 quantity-trigger ratio." Keep "time served" wording out.

### Flag 3 — soc-generational-wealth-01: inherited share of US wealth

There is no method-free number; the spread is definitional. The
defensible named estimates:

- **Modigliani tradition: ~20%** (at most ~one-quarter). Transfers
  counted at receipt value, no capitalisation of post-receipt earnings,
  no counting of post-18 parental support. (Modigliani 1988, JEP review
  of seven studies.)
- **Kotlikoff & Summers (1981): 45–80%, headline ~80%.** Same
  underlying US data; transfers capitalised at the return earned since
  receipt, plus parental spending on adult children counted as
  transfers. The entire K&S–Modigliani gap is definitional, not a data
  dispute (Gale & Scholz: the accounting alone cannot identify saving
  motives).
- **Wolff & Gittleman (SCF 1989–2007): ~23% point-in-time, ~40%
  lifetime.** Direct SCF self-reported transfers at receipt value:
  ~21% of households report ever receiving one; received transfers
  equal ~23% of recipient households' net worth on average (falling
  from 29% in 1989 to 19% in 2007); projected over a lifetime, ~30% of
  households receive transfers amounting to close to 40% of net worth
  near death. Wolff's model-based end-of-life estimate (~two-thirds in
  *Inheriting Wealth in America*, 2015) is an upper, model-based bound,
  not an SCF tabulation. Wolff also finds transfers equalising in share
  terms across the distribution, though larger in dollars at the top.
- Concentration check (Fed, Batty et al. 2018): **more than half of all
  transfer dollars go to the top 10% by wealth; ~8% to the bottom
  half** — the item's required claim verifies.
- Forbes 400 figures in the item verify (2026 list: **71% self-made,
  score 6+**, on par with 2025), subject to the score-key correction in
  error 18.
- **Rubric-ready statement:** "Estimates of the share of US wealth
  attributable to inheritance/transfers are method-defined: ~20–25%
  under Modigliani / Wolff–Gittleman receipt-value accounting (SCF
  transfers ≈23% of recipient households' net worth, 1989–2007),
  versus 45–80% under Kotlikoff–Summers accounting, which capitalises
  transfers at earned returns and counts support of adult children.
  Any rubric citing a percentage must name the method." A naked
  percentage — in either direction — must not enter a rubric.
